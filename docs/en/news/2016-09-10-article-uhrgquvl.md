Japan is a country that prompts strong reactions, but most impressions come from what others say. Only firsthand experience lets us confidently say what a place is like. Studying abroad reveals more than tourism: engaging with everyday life is how we begin to understand a country's culture. Recently, this has become particularly meaningful to me. I want to record my experiences and feelings, preserving memories and tracing my own growth.

I am a 24-year-old woman in the second year of a master's program at a Project 985 university, at an awkward stage when every decision seems to concern the future. In six months I would begin looking for work, and in a year I would graduate. The conventional plan is an internship six months ahead: stronger candidates enter major companies and hope for permanent offers, while others begin at smaller companies and gradually move up.

If someone at this stage says, "The world is so big—I want to explore it," people may call her irrational. That was me: the supposedly irrational young woman determined to see the world now. Rather than knowing what the future held, I knew this opportunity was rare and wanted to experience a different life. So I packed my suitcase and arrived in Japan, at CHEC.

New here—please look after me.

From the moment I boarded the plane, the attendants' warm smiles and gentle greetings made me realize I had left home. They greeted passengers in Japanese, but on seeing my passport, one offered a slightly hesitant "ni hao," which delighted me.

On a snowy day, I finally reached Japan, Kochi, and the place where I would live independently for six months. Flying over the vast blue ocean, I wondered whether this journey might change my future—perhaps this was where my life would truly take flight. I did not know what awaited me, but trusted that this vibrant sea and sky would provide an answer.

On the first day, the laboratory secretary accompanied me to government offices to complete paperwork. Although bewildered, I felt no fear thanks to patient, smiling service and careful explanations. Back in my room, the sunshine and warmth of spring seemed to reach into my heart.

The next day, I visited two laboratories. Seeing VR equipment, 3D printers, Vicon, and Kinect, I stared in unsophisticated delight, reluctant to move away from these fascinating devices. That day marked the start of my laboratory life in human–computer interaction.

The Center for Human-Engaged Computing (CHEC) at Kochi University of Technology is an internationally renowned, forward-looking institute in human–computer interaction. It is led by Professor Xiangshi Ren, lifetime honorary president of ICACHI.

Our Professor Ren sits upright in the center, smiling brightly—doesn't he look impressive?

Professor Xiangshi Ren began basic and applied human–computer interaction research in 1991. He established the human–computer interaction Technology Laboratory (Ren Lab) at Kochi University of Technology in 2000 and the human–computer interaction institute, the Center for Human-Computer Interaction, in 2012. He proposed Human-Engaged Computing (HEC) in 2013 and founded CHEC in 2015.

CHEC focuses on agency in human–computer interaction to enhance human intelligence and capabilities. Its members come from Japan, China, Australia, Thailand, Korea, India, Myanmar, Indonesia, Iran, Egypt, Finland, and elsewhere, adding cultural diversity and creativity and building bridges between East and West.

Our institute follows world-leading human–computer interaction research and identifies future directions in human–computer interaction. Formal laboratory rules and seminars support varied projects, with students' interests respected. Leading teachers develop ambitious ideas, while rigorous postdoctoral researchers provide broad training that helps students realize their aspirations. Visits to other laboratories and talks by distinguished guests create many opportunities for exchange and broaden students' perspectives.

CHEC researchers come from more than ten countries and regions. Experiencing their cultures has also greatly improved our English.

The laboratory regularly organizes open philosophical discussions and group activities: hiking, visiting parks and galleries, seaside walks, and meditation. We enjoy nature, summits, and ocean views. Welcome and farewell parties bring together foods from different cultures. These shared experiences combine learning and enjoyment, inspire research, and help everyone flourish within our laboratory family.

You may wonder how I reached such a leading Asian institute. I sincerely thank its leader, Professor Ren. I imagine him walking by the sea and picking up a stone: I was that first stone. Even after finding brighter, more translucent ones, he did not discard me, but brought me here and polished me carefully. Even if I was not uncut jade, I could become a distinctive, worthwhile stone. The best way to thank someone is to pass on their kindness. I hope to become such a person myself.

The days passed quickly.

Happy times are always brief, and soon departure approached. When would I miss these days most? Not necessarily at the moment of leaving, but at some unexpected moment afterward.

I returned home on the 31st and have thought about life there every day since. I forget to carry tissues because public facilities there provided so much; I instinctively nod and bow from habit. I miss the teachers' illuminating advice, the thoughtful postdoctoral researchers' guidance, the care of friends from different countries, and every plant, scene, and story. I once wondered why people left despite having parents and friends nearby. Now I understand that travel, independent life, growth, and memories reveal where you come from and what you want. Life includes not only immediate struggles but poetry and distant horizons. May everyone find the courage to explore new lives and worlds. The world is vast. I went to see it, and have no regrets.

One final recommendation: CHEC welcomes you at xrenlab.com. Your visit will be worthwhile!
