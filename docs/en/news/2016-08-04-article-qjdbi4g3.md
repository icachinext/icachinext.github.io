## MIT Lets You Truly "Touch" Objects in Video

MIT's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL) has developed Interactive Dynamic Video (IDV), enabling viewers to reach out and manipulate objects in video as though they were real.

IDV relies on the fact that objects vibrate in response to external forces. Researchers record as little as five seconds of video and use an algorithm to predict how the object would vibrate under other forces. Excitingly, no 3D shape reconstruction is required: conventional video and an algorithm suffice to predict a motion model.

## Samsung Note7 Launches with Iris Recognition

Eye-scanning access systems have long been commonplace in films.

Samsung's newly released Note7 supports iris-recognition unlocking. The company chose it for its higher accuracy. Because irises are unique and recognition is relatively resistant to external influences, the technology could eventually replace fingerprints for payments and other functions.

Iris biometrics can read 266 features, compared with 13–60 in other biometric methods. Obtaining a clear iris image requires specialized imaging equipment and user cooperation, generally making theft more difficult than obtaining fingerprints or facial images.

## Google's Project Wing Delivery Drones Begin Testing Soon

Google's drone-delivery project has been public for some time and is finally set for US testing. The White House recently announced initiatives supporting unmanned aircraft research and safety standards, including Google's project.

Project Wing may be the most significant US drone-delivery test to date. On July 26, Amazon announced UK trials, perhaps unwilling to wait for US regulation.

## The World's First Programmable Quantum Computer

Christopher Monroe of the Joint Quantum Institute at the University of Maryland says his team's work introduces the first programmable quantum computer whose settings can be changed through software.

The work builds on decades of trapping and controlling ions. Researchers combine established methods of moving ions with new control and measurement techniques, including laser manipulation and dedicated channels for detecting optical properties.

## Wearable Innovation: A Display That Can Be Applied to Skin

According to the Daily Mail, researchers in KAIST's Department of Materials Science and Engineering have developed a display that adheres to skin and other uneven surfaces without harming the body.

Thin-film transistor (TFT) displays are common in laptops and smartphones. This project uses an ultrathin transparent TFT as the active-matrix backplane of a flexible display. A high-performance oxide TFT array is fabricated on a laser-reactive substrate; irradiation from behind leaves the oxide TFT array.

The ultrathin oxide reaction layer is separated from the substrate and transferred to plastic just four micrometers thick. The transferred oxide drives the circuitry, and the attached TFTs retain up to 83% optical transparency and useful mobility even after severe repeated bending.

## Five Beijing Subway Lines to Adopt Fully Automated Operation

Automated assistance has long supported rail driving; rail transport is now moving toward fully driverless operation.

At a recent Beijing government briefing on refined urban management and emergency support, officials announced that a domestically developed fully automated system would be applied to the Yanfang Line under construction. If successful, it would be China's first urban rail project using an indigenous system requiring no driver or attendant intervention. Trains would wake up, travel between stations, stop precisely, open and close doors, depart, and return to the depot automatically. Fully automated urban rail operation is becoming reality in Beijing.
