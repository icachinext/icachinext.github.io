## Recap: ICACHI Online Forum, Session 4

Session 4 of the ICACHI Online Forum was simulcast by Tencent Wiz and XuetangX on Sunday, July 5, Beijing Time. Organized by International Chinese Association of Computer Human Interaction (ICACHI), the Human-Engaged Computing and Technology for Good series showcases Chinese scholars' shared commitment to combating the pandemic through academic work. As society and the economy slowly restart after the global pandemic's pause, it confronts future challenges and reconsiders technology's social role. It seeks more forward-looking, resilient, and inclusive human–computer interaction models, solutions for known and unknown problems, reflection on progress, and stronger crisis response.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-02.png)

This session addressed Designing Futures: Methods, Tools, and Practice. In a rapidly changing world, exploring future trends, challenges, and opportunities and creating future value through design is a new challenge.

Moving from object-, user-, society-, and nature-centered approaches to future-centered design, this salon brings the future development of all subjects and objects—including humanity—into design's scope and criteria. It introduces futures thinking into design thought and practice. Grounded in design and futures studies, designing futures adds reflection on worldviews and values and helps creators examine present design through possible futures. We also introduce speculative and critical perspectives, discussing concepts, methods, tools, and cases through education and practice. We hope to explore future possibilities through design, inspire more designers to participate, and create desirable futures together.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-03.jpg)

Moderator Qing Xia, a doctoral student at Tsinghua University's Institute of Service Design, opened with brief introductions to ICACHI and the guests: Professor Zhiyong Fu of Tsinghua University, Professor Peter Scupelli of Carnegie Mellon University, and Professor Li Zhang of Guangdong University of Technology. Professor Xiangyang Xin, founder of XXY Innovation's design thinking and strategy consultancy, also joined the panel.

### Zhiyong Fu

Tenured Associate Professor, Department of Information Art and Design, Academy of Arts and Design, Tsinghua University

## Talk Title

Envisioning Future Scenarios—Integrating Futures Studies into Interaction Design Education and Research

Professor Zhiyong Fu began with "The best way to predict the future is to create it," introducing Jim Dator, professor of political science and director of the futures research center at the University of Hawaiʻi. His view is that the future cannot be predicted, but alternative futures can and should be forecast, and preferred futures can and should be envisioned, invented, implemented, continually evaluated, revised, and reevaluated. Many futures are possible; designers must collaborate with experts from other fields and use design to develop futures aligned with human aspirations.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-05.png)

Futures studies is a branch of social science comprehensively examining trends, possible scenarios, challenges, and responses in major areas of human life. It studies larger, more complex world systems and focuses on transformative trends. Four current directions are:

1. Specific forecasting
1. Constructing or simulating seemingly possible or probable alternative futures
1. Studying social imagination and future-making
1. Normative studies and analysis of preferred futures

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-06.png)

Futures studies often enters design through explorations of technological feasibility at major IT companies such as Microsoft. But are technology-shaped futures the ones we need? Humanistic approaches intersect with HCI in several ways:

1. Interaction criticism
1. Critical discourse analysis
1. Critical social science
1. Designing futures
1. Emancipatory human–computer interaction

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-07.png)

Designing futures has a humanistic tendency: fictional near-future prototypes in different media use contemporary design methods to advance understanding and discussion of current projects. Deploying or exhibiting them helps researchers, designers, users, and the public consider near-future sociotechnical possibilities critically and holistically, stimulating debate and gathering support for or opposition to projects.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-08.jpg)

Designing futures differs from design fiction and speculative design through its emphasis on reflective practice and continual revision of possibilities. All three, however, seek a more desirable future.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-09.png)

Professor Zhiyong Fu introduced four tools from futures studies:

1. Futures triangle: the push of the present, including capabilities and trends; the weight of history, including persistent barriers; and the pull of the future, including visions of evolution and progress.
1. Signs of the future: signals in emerging reports, issues experienced firsthand, and trends interpreting those signals and issues.
1. Causal layered analysis: moving beneath appearances through systems, worldviews, and deep subconscious layers.
1. 2×2 scenario planning: defining two axes to create four quadrants for analyzing future scenarios.

These futures tools can be combined with design tools such as customer journey maps.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-10.png)

Finally, Professor Zhiyong Fu introduced two teaching examples applying designing futures, (:PTERIS) and Dream Topia, and discussed bringing these ideas into classrooms by connecting present action, near-term empowerment, and long-term trends.

## Peter Scupelli

Nierenberg Associate Professor in Design, Director of the Learning Environments Lab, School of Design, Carnegie Mellon University

## Talk Title

Dexign Futures for Challenging Times

Professor Peter considered three challenges facing his society in the United States—COVID-19, systemic racism, and sustainability—and how designers should respond. These arise in the Anthropocene, an era of unprecedented human impact on Earth. Design plays a substantial role: from products and services to ways of living, it shapes environments and ecosystems. In confronting major disasters, designers must consider how design can improve conditions and address problems.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-13.png)

People commonly respond to major problems in several ways:

1. Despair: feeling small in the face of challenges can produce hopelessness.
1. Arrogance: blind confidence can create bigger, more complex problems for others to solve.
1. Denial: rejecting the problem and the relevant science, history, and information.

Designers may experience despair, but must acknowledge and overcome it. They should neither become overconfident nor deny problems.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-14.jpg)

How should designers relate design to reality when facing challenges? We often tell stories of hope or fear. Both guide thinking in the short term but can cause problems over time. Unrealistic hope leads to disappointment, while paralyzing fear leads to inaction or even conspiracy theories. Designers should focus on reality and address problems through rigorous research.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-15.png)

We need design that continually changes. Students may find this unusual: designers learn one approach, but when problems change they must develop new methods to address them.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-16.jpg)

Arnold Wasserman's account of design's evolution since 1950 identifies object-centered Design 1.0, emphasizing production and sales; human-centered Design 2.0, emphasizing design thinking; Design 3.0, concerned with social innovation; and Design 4.0, transition design directed toward sustained flourishing for everyone.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-17.jpg)

Elizabeth Pastor offers another humanistic framework: Design 1.0 is traditional design; 2.0 is product and service design; 3.0 concerns organizational transformation; and 4.0 concerns societal transformation. Traditional design thinking works for 1.0 and 2.0, whereas 3.0 and 4.0 require new ways of thinking.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-18.png)

What challenges confront traditional design thinking? Herbert Simon defined design as transforming existing situations into preferred ones. What makes a design preferred? A preferable future emphasizes something better or recommended, a relatively neutral term; designers tend to seek desirable futures that can be experienced. Preferred futures are worthwhile, but designers aspire to desirable ones. Professor Peter identified two issues:

1. Timescales: time is vital to design, yet design cycles are becoming shorter. Software's perpetual beta illustrates products entering the market before completion. Shorter cycles reflect a short-term, unsustainable worldview.
1. Values: historically, aesthetics, intelligence, empowerment, efficiency, effectiveness, and human-centeredness have guided design. New problems require new values and a shift from design as a problem to design as a solution.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-19.jpg)

Dexign replaces the s in design with x to signal a new concept. The UN Sustainable Development Goals should inform designing futures. Long-term design goals require methods from futures studies. Designers must examine future narratives: futures always contain good and bad, but our focus is on preferred and desirable possibilities.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-20.jpg)

Professor Peter concluded with a brief introduction to educational approaches combining design and futures studies.

### Li Zhang

Associate Professor, Department of Industrial Design, School of Art and Design, Guangdong University of Technology

## Talk Title

Discursive Futures and Alternative Things

Professor Li Zhang approached the topic through design theory, moving from speculative and critical design to discursive design and awareness of future risks. Within design, discussing the future has become highly fashionable.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-23.jpg)

In Homo Deus, Yuval Harari introduces the useless class. The Industrial Revolution produced a working class; powerful technologies in the 21st-century AI era may produce a nonworking class whose labor is unnecessary. New technologies could take humanity into unknown, high-risk territory, enhancing physical and cognitive capacities beyond biological limits. Technological optimism sees a utopian vision; a dystopian critique warns that surrendering all decisions to technology and no longer needing to work could create a new useless class.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-24.jpg)

As technology becomes a principal driver of social progress and evolution, we must consider how to preserve fundamental humanity when it transcends, disrupts, or overturns traditional human boundaries. Human-centered design is an important trend in contemporary design theory worldwide. Humanity should return to the center of technological and design ethics.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-25.png)

In technological design, we should consider what machines cannot accomplish: humanity encompasses what cannot be reduced to computation. Professor Li Zhang shared an existentialist understanding of humanity. For Li Zhang, people have no predetermined attributes and manifest differently within different social systems and structures. If an enduring human characteristic exists, it is language: the impulse to use language metaphorically to connect with the world, expressing poetic wisdom in our response to it.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-26.png)

In The Semantic Turn, Klaus Krippendorff traces artifacts' transformation from products to services and commodities, interfaces, systems and networks, projects, and finally discourses. Artifacts increasingly enter the domain of language. His axiom is that people act according to things' meanings for them, rather than their physical properties. Meanings arise through reference, argument, and discussion. Bringing discourse back into design reveals an increasing emphasis on linguistic construction and advancement. In the shift from technological to humanistic determinism, design is no longer fixed; its meaning emerges through language as a medium.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-27.png)

Discursive design uses design to stimulate debate and reflection, providing a vehicle for thought. Human-made things serve not only utilitarian goals but also needs for and the assignment of meaning. Such works have discursive themes that encourage viewers to articulate their own ideas.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-28.png)

Professor Li Zhang then discussed things. Ethical debates can seem dry: abstract concepts expressed in text have barriers to entry and require substantial prior knowledge. Discursive, speculative, and critical design move technological discussion from abstract language to concrete objects. Designed things assume language's role, making ethics tangible and extending technological debate beyond experts to the public.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-29.png)

Alternatives are essential to discursive design. It works by proposing one or more alternative things; without alternatives, meaning does not emerge. If we do not recognize different ways of viewing, understanding, or using something, our understanding remains unconscious and the thing lacks meaningful distinction. Novelty in design and art breaks stereotyped perceptions by offering an alternative.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-30.jpg)

Finally, Li Zhang briefly introduced a discursive design case study.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2020-07-20-recap-forum-31.jpg)

## Panel Discussion

Panelists began by discussing their understanding of designing futures. In Herbert A. Simon's definition of artificial things, all design has some orientation toward the future. What, then, specifically do we mean when discussing designing futures?

They then considered futures studies and design: what challenges arise when combining them?

Finally, the panel reflected on what ideas designing futures can offer amid today's global challenges.
