Author: Xiangshi Ren, Kochi University of Technology

Human-Engaged Computing (HEC) seeks to advance human potential and technological capabilities synergistically, enabling people to realize their full potential and solve complex real-world problems.

The design of human–computer interaction systems should focus on people rather than machines. Otherwise, innovation may be constrained, or, more seriously, lives put at risk—as has already happened.

Several human–computer interaction experts share this view. Bill Buxton has argued that computers should help people realize their full potential. Direct-manipulation pioneer Ben Shneiderman envisions human–computer interaction design advancing individuals and society through trust, empathy, and responsibility. Terry Winograd and Gary Bradski have identified AI's limitations in natural language processing and computer vision respectively, proposing a shift toward strengthening human abilities.

Despite such calls, researchers have not fully explored how information technology can help people realize their potential. Instead, it is mainly used to perform tasks more efficiently and productively. This does not necessarily enhance people; automation often creates problems, including greater time pressure in decision-making.

Stephen Hawking, Bill Gates, and Elon Musk have expressed concern about superintelligent AI's implications for humanity's development and survival. Public concerns are similar. Accenture's 2016 survey of 28,000 people in 28 countries found technology fatigue and particular anxiety about security and privacy.

Does digital development serve or harm humanity? Are we simply consumers sustaining technology companies, or should technology help us realize our full potential? These questions are critical to our future.

Computing to Enhance Human Potential

In 2006, human–computer interaction pioneer Douglas Engelbart wrote to me in person that future human–computer interaction should focus on increasing human capabilities to develop, integrate, and understand the knowledge needed to improve society's chances of survival. This prompted me to rethink the relationship between people and computers.

Pioneers such as Engelbart and J. C. R. Licklider neither overlooked innate human potential nor rejected technological progress. In 1960, Licklider wrote that people would set goals and evaluate results, while computers would handle technical and scientific problem-solving to implement those insights and decisions. At the 1995 Vannevar Bush symposium, Engelbart similarly argued that both sides—people and machines—must advance together through coordinated evolution.

Existing broad accounts of human–computer interaction have not fully captured this co-evolution. Human-centered computing emphasizes users' needs but often becomes a matter of convenience and gratification. User experience considers feelings and values, yet its industrial-design origins can keep it technology-driven. MIT's Center for Collective Intelligence (http://cci.mit.edu) asks how people and computers can work more intelligently together, but intelligence is only one aspect of human potential.

Advancing Human and Technological Potential Through Organic Synergy

To develop the wisdom needed for complex real-world problems, human–computer interaction researchers must advance human and technological capabilities together. Effective co-evolution requires identifying human potential and technological constraints, realizing those capabilities, and developing technologies that enhance them further.

Eastern philosophers see enormous, sometimes immeasurable, human potential. Traditional human–computer interaction often limits it to cognition and motor control. Recent research suggests innate intelligence alone does not ensure excellence: concentration, mindfulness, self-control, autonomy, empathy, and trust may matter more. Fully synergistic human–computer interaction can occur only when systems appropriately recognize all individual and collective capabilities.

Technological advances can accompany declines in human abilities. Long-term use of electronic address books may weaken memory, while electronic devices can hinder concentration. A recent Microsoft study suggested that human attention spans had fallen below those of goldfish. Continued decline in our own capabilities would severely constrain deeper synergy in human–computer interaction.

The first step toward a good computing system is to integrate multidisciplinary evidence, comprehensively assessing and prioritizing human potential and the technological barriers to realizing it **.** Neuroscience and positive psychology, for example, can help human–computer interaction researchers evaluate the costs and benefits of enhancing intelligence through technology: greater distraction or difficulty regulating emotion may coexist with improved multitasking. For autonomous driving, researchers must likewise examine when yielding control to AI benefits or disadvantages people, starting from human outcomes.

After assessing human capabilities comprehensively, human–computer interaction researchers can integrate them with technology to maximize symbiosis [r1] and minimize conflict. The brain is plastic and continuously adapts. By using appropriate technologies to change experience, human–computer interaction designers can preserve, enhance, or restore abilities. Meditation applications such as Headspace (www.headspace.com) and Smiling Mind (http://smilingmind.com.au) may reduce stress and improve concentration and self-control. Crowdsourcing can mobilize collective potential to build trust, empathy, and community. Sometimes, however, human potential is better realized without technological intervention.

HUMAN-ENGAGED COMPUTING

I call this organic co-evolution of human and technological capabilities Human-Engaged Computing (HEC). As Figure 1 illustrates, it comprises engaged humans, engaging computers, and synergized interaction.

Engaged Humans

Human engagement occurs as our inner capabilities develop and become fully involved in an activity. Fundamentally, engagement does not depend on whether the activity is interesting, difficult, or beneficial. It is wholehearted immersion. Eastern traditions associate this energetic concentration with meditation and self-transcendence; Western psychology describes it as achieving flow or being in the zone. Pure human engagement cannot be produced by external incentives such as gratification, because these can create distraction.

Engaging Computers

Computers, including hardware and software, also engage in enhancing human capabilities. HEC technologies are therefore judged not simply by conventional quantitative or qualitative measures such as productivity or enjoyment, but by their capacity to realize human potential.

A central design challenge is deciding what to do when technology strengthens one capability while weakening another. Certain games may improve children's short-term learning while eroding intrinsic motivation. Should calculators mean we stop teaching basic mathematics? Is it wise to let thinking, spelling, writing, collaboration, and other skills decline because computers can perform them? What happens when children encounter complex problems requiring both human and technological capabilities? HEC raises public awareness by continually asking such questions.

Synergized Interaction

Synergized interaction is the optimal balance of engaged humans and engaging computers during capability enhancement, reflecting an Eastern concept discussed in the accompanying Human-Engaged Computing: Getting the Balance Right. In this balance, computing serves more than predetermined objectives such as faster task completion. Technology develops and enhances existing and latent human abilities to achieve higher-level, even as-yet-undefined goals. Depending on the circumstances, synergy need not involve sophisticated technology.

Better human–computer collaboration requires a holistic view of human capabilities and technological barriers. Drawing on social science, the humanities, and different areas of human–computer interaction can help us design systems that realize human potential more fully and create a better world.

Human-Engaged Computing: Getting the Balance Right

human–computer interaction has developed through four waves. The first, rooted in industrial engineering and ergonomics, optimized the fit between people and machines. The second drew on cognitive psychology and emphasized similarities in information processing. The third turned toward people, including the social and emotional dimensions of human–computer interaction. The current fourth wave combines positive psychology and cognitive neuroscience to consider well-being, creativity, emotion, ethics, and self-actualization, offering a more human-centered perspective.

HEC draws on the fourth wave but focuses on engaging people to realize their greatest potential. It combines the human–computer collaboration advocated by J. C. R. Licklider and Douglas Engelbart in the 1960s with balanced moderation in Chinese philosophy, expressed in the I Ching and Confucian works such as the Doctrine of the Mean. Here balance means complementarity: the partners together achieve more than either alone. For human–computer interaction, people and computers should receive attention proportionate to their contribution to overall harmony, rather than mechanically equal treatment. Ignoring these proportions creates imbalance, wasting resources on one side while neglecting the other. Balanced moderation rarely means numerically equal expenditure, although both sides remain essential in allocating resources. The decisive criterion is how best to enhance human potential.

Eastern influences on HEC also appear in holism, conceptions of happiness, meditation, and concentration—topics attracting companies such as Apple, Facebook, and Google. HEC thus brings Eastern thought and practice into computing.

Other fields have successfully combined Eastern and Western approaches, including yoga psychotherapy, Chinese medicine, and business management. Joining Eastern holism with Western analysis may offer effective ways to use computing to meet human needs.

About the Author

Xiangshi Ren is a professor and doctoral supervisor in the School of Information at Kochi University of Technology (Kochi University of Technology), Japan, and director of its HEC research center. He founded International Chinese Association of Computer Human Interaction (ICACHI) and serves as lifetime honorary president. He was among China Association for Science and Technology's first overseas Haizhi Program experts, is a Ministry of Education Chunhui Program expert and overseas director of the Jilin Overseas Exchange Association, and is a senior member of ACM and IEEE.
