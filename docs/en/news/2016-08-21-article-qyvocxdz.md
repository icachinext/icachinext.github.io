Forbes identifies five major technologies that could transform the next 15 years: **new computing architectures**, **genomics**, **nanotechnology**, **energy storage**, and **robotics**. As these technologies reinforce one another and develop together, humanity will begin to reshape the physical world.

Fifteen years ago, in 2001, only 5% of the world's population used the Internet, and connections were slow; today the figure is 50%. Mobile phones were widespread but mainly used for calls and texts, smartphones were still distant, and Google was only a startup.

Even online, people mostly exchanged email and accessed basic services. YouTube was only five years old, e-commerce had not yet been widely accepted as a viable business model, many doubted Amazon would survive, and social media was not widespread.

Technological progress since then has been remarkable. The next 15 years may be even more astonishing. While past advances largely transformed the virtual world, by 2030 we may see technology reshape the physical world.

## 1. New Computing Architectures

In 1965, Gordon Moore formulated the prediction that chip processing capabilities would double every 18 months. Engineers sustained this trajectory for 50 years, but Moore's Law is approaching its limits. Three-dimensional stacking and FPGA technologies may extend it somewhat, but fundamental progress requires new computing architectures.

One approach is quantum computing, using superposition and entanglement to build chips potentially millions of times more powerful. Another is neuromorphic computing, imitating the brain with processing speeds potentially billions of times faster than conventional computers.

Commercial deployment remains years away, but prototypes already exist. Within perhaps a decade, these architectures could transform computing.

## 2. Genomics

Sequencing the first human genome in 2003 cost more than US$3 billion. By 2030, improved sequencing could cost less than US$100.

Genomics already supports medicine, including treatments for breast cancer based on patients' genetic profiles. By 2030, it may combine with immunotherapy to activate the immune system against cancer and make the disease curable.

CRISPR also enables precise gene editing and the creation of organisms with useful functions in cellular factories. Inserting suitable genes into bacteria and other microorganisms could even enable them to produce plastics.

## 3. Nanotechnology

In 1959, physicist Richard Feynman proposed producing materials at the atomic level, then a seemingly science-fictional idea. Today, graphene and quantum dots are opening new possibilities in materials.

The potential applications are extensive, although molecular-level materials development is still at an early stage. By 2030, downloading designs for new physical materials could become as commonplace as downloading software today.

Following Feynman's vision, nanotechnology could also produce nanoscale medicines. Devices smaller than a human hair could treat individual diseased cells more effectively.

By 2030, nanorobots could travel through the bloodstream to locate diseased cells and pathogens or repair specific cells.

## 4. Energy Storage

Energy storage has advanced for 40 years. Since lithium-ion batteries appeared in 1970, energy density and costs have increased; in some laptops, batteries account for as much as 90% of the weight and volume. The mobile-device revolution has pushed batteries toward smaller sizes and lower costs. Yet, like Moore's Law for chips, lithium-ion technology is approaching theoretical limits. Researchers seek alternatives. At Argonne National Laboratory, a new generation of batteries is being developed with five times the capacity at one-fifth the cost.

## 5. Robotics

Robotics is another rapidly developing field. Previously, robots were used almost exclusively in heavy industry, separated from people for safety. Today they increasingly work alongside people in factories and on battlefields.

By 2030, robots could play a larger role in everyday life. Nanomaterials may make them lighter and stronger, while powerful neuromorphic chips and advanced deep-learning algorithms could enable natural interaction with people.

Digital technologies largely defined the past 15 years. The next 15 may be characterized by deep integration across technologies.

More powerful computing architectures will enable genomic and molecular work and ultimately intelligent machines. New energy sources and storage technologies will make these systems more practical, safe, and efficient.

In 2016, we have gained command of the digital world. By 2030, we may begin to reshape the physical world.

## Editor's Note:

Following the discussion of robotics, I want to emphasize human–computer interaction. My undergraduate degree was in measurement and control technology and instrumentation, a sensor-oriented field. I once declared that the future belonged to sensors. Sensors are indeed machines' only direct means of perceiving the environment, like human senses. But machines ultimately serve people. As human factors grow in importance, communication between people and machines becomes more complex. If we imagine machines as people, earlier interactions resembled dealing with nature in a primitive society, while today's resemble social relationships in a civilization. This makes human–computer interaction particularly important.

Why is human–computer interaction not considered mainstream or widely understood? I see three reasons: human–computer interaction is perceived as new, the methods used in human–computer interaction research, and the extraordinary breadth of human–computer interaction.

- Is human–computer interaction really new? We have used the mouse and designed interfaces for decades, so human–computer interaction is hardly recent. Yet compared with computer science, sensing, and communications technologies, human–computer interaction can seem comparatively young.
- As for human–computer interaction methods, I mean particularly mathematical methods. Fitts' Law immediately became central to human–computer interaction, and many researchers continued its probabilistic and statistical approach. This is not a criticism of statistics; as an undergraduate, I even thought that if God existed, God must be probability. Yet statistical approaches can be black boxes: we use them because we do not know what happens inside. This uncertainty can create discrepancies between research and real outcomes and make results less persuasive.
- The scope of human–computer interaction is also exceptionally broad. I have always seen human–computer interaction as interdisciplinary, combining human psychology, computer sensing, and many other technologies. Any small branch could occupy an entire research career.

How do we move forward? Broad exploration with focused breakthroughs seems a sound approach. human–computer interaction cannot advance in one leap; it must evolve continually. May the "brain" and "body" of human–computer interaction develop together.
