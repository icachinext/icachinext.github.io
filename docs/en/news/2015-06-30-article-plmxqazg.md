## X-Studio

Tsinghua University

In 2015, CHI came to Asia for the first time. More than a dozen students from X-Studio at Tsinghua University took this opportunity to attend the leading international conference in human–computer interaction for the first time. As newcomers to HCI research, they learned diligently, exchanged ideas widely, and gained a great deal over these few days. They also recorded their experiences and reflections.

—Editor's Note

When our plane landed at Beijing Capital International Airport on the 25th, the X-Studio students' six-day CHI journey came to an end.

X-Studio also contributed to Chinese CHI this year, presenting five papers in the Interactive Arts & Design session. We therefore arrived in Seoul early, on April 17, to prepare our presentations. Chinese CHI brought together Chinese participants from around the world. Seeing a room full of familiar-looking faces felt reassuring, but discovering that everyone was speaking English still created some pressure for us as first-time international conference attendees.

During a break between the morning talks, we put our coordinated posters on the display boards while other participants were already presenting theirs. The poster session was an important part of the event, helping attendees get to know one another. We met researchers from many institutions and students from Taiwan. Everyone was enthusiastic, and the atmosphere was lively and friendly.

Five presentations in the afternoon Interactive Arts & Design session came from X-Studio, perhaps reflecting our sustained exploration of the integration of design and technology. Afterward, many students came specifically to our posters for further discussion. We were also delighted that a paper by Zhang Yingxue from Professor Yuanchun Shi's studio, also at Tsinghua University, received the Chinese CHI Best Paper Award.

Although Chinese CHI was not a large event, it enabled us to meet leading Chinese human–computer interaction researchers face to face and connect with our peers. Talking with people from different backgrounds and learning about their worlds and ideas helped inspire our work. This was our greatest gain from Chinese CHI.

After Chinese CHI, the main CHI conference began.

For many of us, this was our first international academic conference. Before arriving, we had not realized how extraordinary the experience would be. Encountering CHI without those expectations made its impact even stronger.

More than 3,000 people registered for CHI: nearly a third came from the United States and Canada, nearly a third from Korea, and the remainder from other countries. There were 108 registrations from China, excluding on-site registrations on opening day. The vast opening venue was full of people whose varied appearances reflected their origins around the world. Everyone carried red conference bags and wore red badge lanyards. That shared red linked us, symbolizing our belonging to one community for these few days.

We knew we were sharing the room with the world's leading human–computer interaction researchers and imagined a very formal gathering. Yet almost nobody wore a suit; most dressed casually, and even the conference chair appeared on stage without formal attire. The atmosphere was approachable, relaxed, and natural, while remaining courteous and professional. To me, this is the ideal academic atmosphere: even first-time attendees need not feel constrained or unsure of themselves.

The four-day program was packed, with dozens of thematic sessions running simultaneously in each time slot. Each session contained four to six papers, with talks lasting 10–20 minutes. Since hearing everything was impossible, we began planning as soon as we received the program, two days before CHI. We identified topics of interest, read the corresponding papers, watched their videos, and selected the most worthwhile sessions. Our more experienced colleagues helped considerably. On the evening before CHI, they held a small meeting in our guesthouse living room to introduce the themes of the 2015 papers and recommend sessions to the younger students.

With this preparation, we no longer felt lost at the conference and moved confidently from one session to the next.

At the end of the first day, the organizers hosted a reception with plentiful food and traditional Korean performances. Even more appealing was the opening of the exhibition. Compared with talks, demonstrations were more immediately engaging and posters more concise. Dozens of booths displayed intriguing projects, including chocolate 3D printers, while hundreds of posters competed for our attention. There was more than we could take in at once. Fortunately, the exhibition continued for several days, giving us time to explore it carefully.

From the second day onward, the talks became increasingly unconventional: presenters performed magic, ate while speaking, and invited the audience to dance Gangnam Style. Such freedom and playfulness at a leading academic gathering showed how imaginative, unconventional people use computing to enrich life's possibilities and appeal.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2015-06-30-article-plmxqazg-02.png)

A talk during CHI

One memorable detail was the Local Heroes display we saw immediately upon entering the exhibition on the first day. Each large board featured an Asian scholar who had made outstanding contributions to human–computer interaction. Professor Yuanchun Shi's board stood prominently near the entrance. We were delighted and immediately took a group photograph. Over the following days, our large group dispersed to attend different sessions, but we always reunited in front of Professor Shi's board.

"I've just come out of Room 301. Where is everyone?"
 "Meet at the landmark—we're all here!"
 Professor Shi's "landmark" certainly helped keep our group together.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2015-06-30-article-plmxqazg-03.png)

Group photograph at the landmark

Every evening, we gathered in our guesthouse living room to share what we had learned, discuss ideas, or simply chat and play games. Our host even prepared snacks. We debated questions enthusiastically and often laughed together over amusing experiences. It was warm and cheerful, like a close extended family.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2015-06-30-article-plmxqazg-04.png)

Our nightly gathering in the guesthouse living room

Everyone felt that, despite the succession of talks, the program never dragged. Sessions always seemed to end quickly, and before we quite realized it, the four-day conference was nearly over.

The closing ceremony traditionally includes awards, a conference summary, and a keynote by a distinguished scholar. This year's closing speaker, however, was an unexpected figure: Korean pop star PSY.

We were all curious about what PSY's academic talk would be like. In contrast to his eccentric music-video persona, an impeccably composed and elegant PSY stepped onto the stage and described his musical career in fluent English.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2015-06-30-article-plmxqazg-05.png)

PSY delivering the closing keynote

Although more serious than his music videos, PSY's talk was full of stories and humor that repeatedly had the audience laughing. It did not explore human–computer interaction in academic depth, but his self-deprecation and self-analysis revealed a clear-headed person and offered insight into his success. His experiences were inspiring in their own right. Electronic music and social media helped make his success a product of the information age, while his keynote embodied CHI's openness to diverse fields. Suddenly, it was hard to imagine anyone better suited to this closing keynote before thousands of attendees. He won over most of this academic audience, who sent him off with enthusiastic applause.

June 27, 2015

Finally, the CHI 2015 chair stepped down, and the CHI 2016 organizers took the stage. The screen's logo changed from 2015 to 2016, and red gave way to blue and purple. CHI 2016 had issued its invitation: see you in California, USA, in 2016!
