In 2012, Oculus raised US$2.5 million on Kickstarter and developed its first prototype. Later reports suggested that the campaign was not profitable, but it attracted public attention and laid the groundwork for the company's acquisition in 2014. Facebook's US$2 billion purchase gave the long-established technology of VR fresh momentum. Over the next two years, consumer electronics manufacturers rapidly introduced VR and AR products. Some predict an explosion of VR in 2016 and call it the first year of VR; we shall see.

Today, we look at the different manufacturers' approaches to VR.

## Immersive VR

Oculus Rift

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-05-02-article-ii4bmax0-01.jpg)

After two developer kits, DK1 and DK2, Oculus released the consumer Rift. Its two eyepieces provide a combined resolution of 2160×1200, or 1080×1200 per eye, with a 110-degree field of view and 90 Hz refresh rate. It retains head tracking, adds Touch controllers and their sensors, and supports Xbox controllers. Oculus has also introduced Oculus Home, an app-store-like one-stop service for its devices.

Resolution and refresh rate both improve on the DK2's 1920×1080 and 75 Hz. The higher refresh rate substantially reduces visual latency. The Constellation tracking system uses periodic infrared emissions from the headset, detected by an infrared camera, at intervals of approximately one to two minutes. These details were not in the official documentation; the author learned them through considerable trial and error and hopes they will be useful.

The biggest change is the Touch controllers and accompanying sensors. Released videos suggest that they likewise emit infrared signals. Controllers greatly improve playability: rather than simply watching a VR film, users can naturally manipulate objects in the virtual world. Third-party devices such as Leap Motion and Kinect previously enabled some applications, but these remained limited. Touch therefore marks a major step forward in Oculus interaction.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-05-02-article-ii4bmax0-02.jpg)

Touch controllers and their sensors

Oculus Home naturally becomes a hub for VR games and films, at least for Oculus users. Its store connects developers and consumers in a healthy ecosystem. Home also provides a one-stop setup, replacing cumbersome driver and runtime installations. In the author's experience, however, support for older devices remains limited; possible unofficial solutions can be found independently online.

HTC Vive

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-05-02-article-ii4bmax0-03.jpg)

Former smartphone leader HTC has turned to VR. Vive offers 2160×1200 resolution, or 1080×1200 per eye, a 110-degree field of view, and 90 Hz refresh—broadly similar to Oculus Rift. Its software offerings include its own Viewport platform and a partnership with Steam as a SteamVR product.

Users who have tried both often describe HTC Vive as more immersive. This former consumer electronics giant should not be underestimated. Its standout feature is the Lighthouse tracking system.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-05-02-article-ii4bmax0-04.jpg)

Diagram of the Lighthouse tracking system

According to ifanr:

Lighthouse uses Valve's patented technology. Rather than cameras, it relies on lasers and photosensors to locate moving objects. Two laser emitters are placed at opposite corners of an adjustable 15-by-15-foot area. Two fixed rows of LEDs inside the emitters produce beams six times per second, scanning the space back and forth.

The Vive headset and controllers contain more than 70 photosensors. As the laser scans, the headset starts timing like a stopwatch, detecting which sensor receives the beam first. Combining sensor positions with arrival times gives an accurate location relative to the emitters. With enough sensors illuminated, a 3D model can determine both headset position and orientation.

## “Mobile VR”

Gear VR

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-05-02-article-ii4bmax0-05.png)

Samsung Gear VR, developed with Oculus, combines VR with mobility and finally removes the long cable. Gear VR costs US$99 and Gear VR 2 around US$200, much less than the US$500–600 devices above. First, however, you need a Samsung S-series phone.

Gear VR's specifications largely depend on the phone. The Samsung S6 provides a 2K display at 2560×1440, while the article reports a default Android refresh rate of 65 Hz and a 65-degree viewing angle.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-05-02-article-ii4bmax0-06.jpg)

The headset's built-in touchpad and buttons

Gear VR supports two forms of human–computer interaction: the headset's built-in touchpad and buttons, or a Samsung or third-party Bluetooth controller.

Google CARDBOARD

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-05-02-article-ii4bmax0-07.jpg)

Google's 20-percent-time policy lets employees devote a fifth of their working time to independent projects. Google Cardboard emerged from this policy. David Coz, an employee in Google's French office, initially developed this foldable smartphone headset from lenses, magnets, hook-and-loop fasteners, and a rubber band. A phone running stereoscopic software displays separate left and right images through the lenses, producing a 3D experience.

The cardboard viewer connects with the phone in two ways: NFC for plug-and-play operation, or a scanned QR code supplying device information.

Its principles resemble Gear VR, but its construction is simpler. Google offers the Cardboard application for iOS and Android and provides an SDK for developers.
