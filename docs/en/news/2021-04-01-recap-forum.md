The Chinese-language forum Humanity, Technology, and the Future: Awareness, Responsibility, and Action—Reflections on the Post-AI Era took place on the evening of March 21. It was initiated by International Chinese Association of Computer Human Interaction's Young Scholars Committee and the Innovation Promotion Committee of the Chinese Professors Association in Japan, with support from Kochi University of Technology's Human-Engaged Computing institute and the China–Japan Young Industry–Academia Federation. Lasting around four hours, it attracted more than 2,000 online viewers.

## Forum Recap

Three moderators, seven senior university and industry researchers, and fourteen doctoral students or young researchers worldwide discussed future technological development. Professor Xiangshi Ren of Japan's Kochi University of Technology opened with a keynote on Human-Engaged Computing. Moderated by Professor Qun Jin of Waseda University, senior presentations followed from Yingqing Xu of Tsinghua University; Professor Di Liu of Kyorin University; Professor Jianmin Wang of Tongji University; Dr. Liang Shi, principal researcher at Mitsui Chemicals; Professor Wei Liu of Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications; Dr. Jianmin Jin, principal researcher at Fujitsu; and Dr. Wei Li, chief scientist of Huawei's human–computer interaction Laboratory. Drawing on their backgrounds, they discussed future technological problems, developments, and opportunities. The fourteen young presenters addressed sensing, intelligent systems, robot interaction, accessibility, mental health, and related topics.

Professor Zixue Cheng of the University of Aizu moderated the Humanity, Technology, and the Future panel. Experts praised the young researchers' work and anticipated new directions exploring how technology can support and expand human spirituality, emotion, mutual trust, and aesthetics. They also raised familiar AI questions: what if AI develops harmfully, and does unequal technological capability imply social inequality? Fujitsu principal researcher Jianmin Jin argued that intelligence development must address how decision-making power is distributed within and outside systems, making governance and standards paramount. Professor Di Liu of Kyorin University offered a humanistic view: a good society must be fair and just. When profit maximization creates imbalance, ethics becomes grounded in modern capitalist assumptions. We should therefore examine and revise current ethical standards as well as considering future technological ethics.

Discussion repeatedly returned to the technological ethics we need. Before ethics, however, questions about what systems we can design and whether they might surpass people inevitably raise the nature of intelligence. Professor Wei Liu of Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications explained that current intelligent systems largely reflect Western mathematical thinking, relying on deterministic inputs and outputs and programmable processing. For Professor Wei Liu, intelligence combines objective facts with subjective values. Systems should be open and dynamic, potentially use little or no data, and involve uncertain inputs and outputs and only partially programmable processing. Such uncertainty prevents mathematics from being an absolute foundation. Machines handle complicated structure well, while humans better manage complex contextual variation: flexible understanding of macro- and micro-level causality, described in Chinese as strategic reckoning. Used neutrally, this concept makes intelligence possible, and current research is exploring computational strategic reckoning.

Returning from broad technological development to research practice, Yingqing Xu of Tsinghua University shared educational reflections on exploratory, zero-to-one research. Interdisciplinary integration creates opportunities, but he also urged students to observe and think about everyday people and objects—for example, the placement of elevator controls—and understand their design logic. Deeper reflection reveals opportunities and needs. Both thought and action matter in topic selection; whatever the starting point, researchers should avoid a vague overall understanding and recognize their work's value through publication at progressively stronger venues. Dr. Wei Li of Huawei's human–computer interaction Laboratory added the importance of identifying genuine problems and welcomed students to encounter industry's many practical challenges.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2021-04-01-recap-forum-05.png)

Professor Xiangshi Ren concluded by explaining the title Reflections on the Post-AI Era. We need not fear AI surpassing humans: even if it occurs, J. C. R. Licklider's prediction places it a century to five centuries away, because human–computer symbiosis and synergy are only beginning. While everyone discusses AI, we should think ahead to what comes after the excitement or after AI becomes routine. Technological problems will eventually be solved. Rather than asking how strongly technology will affect our future, we should ask what future people want and what technologies we wish to create for human benefit.

We hope to renew our thinking at a deeper level: first understand human nature, then reconsider whether past experiences, methods, and views match the future values we seek. As a co-organizer, the ICACHI Young Scholars Committee will continue offering meaningful, thought-provoking activities. We look forward to meeting again.
