By Zuoyun Yang, University of Science and Technology Beijing

In this golden autumn, I was fortunate to accept Professor Xiangshi Ren's invitation to attend IxAP 2016—Interactive Technology and Ageing Populations—at Kochi University of Technology (Kochi University of Technology), Japan. Experts from as many as 18 countries shared ideas for advancing human–computer interaction technologies for older adults. As a first-year student, I felt especially fortunate. It was my first international conference of this scale, first solo trip abroad, first visit to Japan, and first time communicating in English with people from so many countries. These many firsts left unforgettable memories.

## Bustling Yet Peaceful

Kochi's fresh air and quiet surprised me. Airports and shopping centers lacked the expected crowds and noise; people did not shout or make loud phone calls. I heard no car horns during my four-day stay. This peaceful countryside seemed ideal for reflection and concentrated study. One evening, we visited Obiyamachi, a pedestrian shopping district. Most shops closed by nine, leaving mainly restaurants. Even the narrowest alleys were clean and free of clutter.

My teacher explained that this was suburban Kochi. Fields surrounded neat, carefully built houses with low walls or none at all. Roadside drains carried clear water without rubbish or silt. Everywhere offered a pleasing view.

People were consistently courteous and welcoming, greeting strangers in lifts. When asked for directions, they explained enthusiastically and sometimes personally accompanied anyone who did not understand. Many taxi drivers were gray-haired older men, yet readily stepped out to open doors and handle luggage.

## Thoughtful Yet Engaging

An international conference is an excellent opportunity to broaden one's horizons. human–computer interaction participants included established leaders, emerging scholars, entrepreneurs, and graduate students like me. Although our interests differed, we shared an understanding that population aging is an increasingly serious challenge and that human–computer interaction should help address it. Aging seems distant while we are young, but as Professor Frank explained, many bodily functions begin declining while we still consider ourselves youthful.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-10-24-conference-03.jpg)

During the lunchtime poster session, authors courteously invited questions and answered them with enthusiasm and care. Their words and expressions revealed a genuine interest in their work. Most were young researchers only a few years older than me, making conversations relaxed and allowing me to appreciate their dedication and persistence.

Slides are an important means of academic communication, and good slides help researchers convey ideas clearly. Presenters balanced images and concise text; keywords and illustrations aided understanding even when spoken details were missed. Carefully prepared animations and experiment videos made their work vivid. We should learn not only to conduct experiments but also to explain and present them. In flash talks, speakers introduced their teams and research, revealing each group's character and energy. Research involves not just rigorous logic and data but also the pleasure of discussion and mutual support.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-10-24-conference-04.jpg)

## Delicious Yet Healthy

The conference offered Japanese and French dishes and many small treats. We also visited an Asahi brewery and tasted its rich Super Dry beer. Meals were generally not oily; many involved raw ingredients and dipping sauces, making them both delicious and healthy. I cannot recall every dish's name or method of eating, so the photographs can speak for themselves.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-10-24-conference-05.jpg)

## Varied Yet Meticulous

Each day was so full that it seemed to disappear in an instant. Every detail had been considered: volunteers guiding transfers, snacks during breaks and on buses, laboratory visits between discussions, and a relaxing brewery and shopping trip. Research deserves the same care. Reading Work with Heart recently reminded me that even ordinary work can produce unexpected results when done more accurately, quickly, thoughtfully, and wholeheartedly, applying one's intelligence to find new methods. Carelessness may fool others briefly, but never one's conscience or future. Successful people strive for the best in every task, whatever their occupation. We may imagine ourselves destined for great things and unsuited to small details, but how can we accomplish the former without mastering the latter? As Hilton said, there are no humble occupations, only humble people.

My brief Kochi journey has ended, but I will not forget earnest discussions of technology and aging, songs on the bus, being among a magician's first audience, mist around distant mountains, or greenery beside a quarry. Words cannot capture all those impressions and beauty, but everything from this trip will remain a treasured memory.
