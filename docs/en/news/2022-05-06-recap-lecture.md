## Lecture Recap

## Joseph A. Konstan

## Joseph A. Konstan

Professor Konstan explained that human-centered artificial intelligence (HCAI) goes beyond simple machine learning to solve users' problems more intelligently. Rapid advances in computing hardware and AI algorithms have encouraged research on adapting AI applications to human needs and preferences, including explainable AI and human-controlled intelligent systems.

Professor Konstan Then Introduced Two Research Areas:

**Research Area 1: Human-Centered Recommender Systems**

## Human-Centered Recommender Systems

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2022-05-06-recap-lecture-03.png)

Recommender systems are widely used for email filtering, product reviews, and personalized suggestions. Often framed as missing-value prediction, they use large, multidimensional datasets to train effective predictive models. This works well for short-term needs but less well for long-term goals such as exploration, retention, and satisfaction. Konstan proposed three steps: identify different evaluation metrics; identify useful factors such as serendipity and diversity; and develop algorithms considering both. Solutions depend on context: for search engines, serendipity matters less because short- and long-term needs largely coincide. His current work includes productive flyby users, modeling rating disposition, and balancing short- and long-term needs.

## Research Area 2: Organizational Communication

## Organizational Communications

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2022-05-06-recap-lecture-04.png)

Universities and companies send many lengthy announcements and newsletters. Recipients spend time finding relevant information or ignore them. Filtering and personalization can reduce recipients' costs while increasing senders' effort. Balancing both sides' communication costs, employees' needs, and organizational priorities is a multistakeholder optimization problem. Konstan's research develops an economic model considering sender, recipient, and transmission costs and the organization's net benefit. Further dimensions include visibility and better sending and receiving strategies.

Konstan concluded with two HCAI challenges: it extends beyond computing into psychology, economics, business analytics, and design; and it requires not simply more data, but more useful data informed by studying human behavior.

## Discussion Recap

### Dakuo Wang

## Dakuo Wang

Principal researcher at the MIT–IBM Watson AI Lab, visiting professor at Stanford, adjunct professor at Northeastern, ACM Distinguished Speaker, and IEEE senior member. Research interests include human–computer interaction, human–AI collaboration, and human-centered AI systems, with over 50 papers and 60 patents.

**1. Dakuo: Recommender systems are highly technical and algorithmic. How do they connect with human–computer interaction (HCI) or human-centered approaches?**

**Joseph:** Recommender systems originated in collaborative filtering. The first six or seven papers came from different human–computer interaction research groups and were written by human–computer interaction pioneers. Collaborative filtering was initially conceived as something people could do manually: Xerox PARC's Tapestry created a shared database of opinions. The first automated systems—Ringo, GroupLens, and Video Recommender—also came from prominent human–computer interaction groups. Those early systems concerned computer-supported cooperative work or overlapping AI research.

The field then changed, driven partly by large datasets. An early dataset from the DEX Systems research center contained around 2.3 million movie ratings, followed by the Netflix dataset, later withdrawn because of problems. The Netflix Prize offered US$1 million for the best algorithm improving its recommender's accuracy by 10%, encouraging widespread algorithmic work. Human-centered recommendation, however, has always been our goal. It may not have remained central to the field, but many young researchers are now returning to it as a core topic. I appreciate the question and welcome discussion: historically, this was always a human–computer interaction (HCI) problem.

**2. Dakuo: What advice do you have for younger students wishing to participate in ACM?**

**Joseph:** Start locally. Join local or national societies and clubs, attend nearby events, and volunteer at conferences. Volunteers may help speakers directly, a valuable opportunity. Then consider whether you wish to become a professional researcher. If so, attend early-stage workshops in your field or help organize one. Begin with small things; do them well, and people will invite you to do more. Build your involvement steadily, step by step.

**3. Dakuo: Global divisions are widening. As ACM's prospective president, how would you bridge these gaps and reconnect the world, particularly helping Chinese students and scholars participate?**

**Joseph:** There are interesting challenges. We have learned what can work remotely and what does not. Video access to talks is valuable, but good online conferences require more than a meeting link. We should share their most valuable elements online and arrange talks and conversations into a fuller experience. Other fields offer models, such as a major event showcasing the year's highlights. The aim is to bring resources together and encourage exchange. Federations of local events can also connect regions around a theme, letting groups present local work, compare perspectives, propose solutions, and receive feedback. I envision a diverse community whose groups collaborate and advance together.

**4. Dakuo: Intelligent recommendations may negatively affect human abilities, including decision-making. How can we maintain a balance from a human–computer interaction perspective?**

**Joseph:** Nearly all intelligent systems now involve recommendations. Calculators may weaken arithmetic skills, yet we realize that focusing on mathematics rather than arithmetic can produce better results. Recommenders should help identify what is harmful and discourage or encourage actions accordingly. If a computer always chooses my restaurant, am I losing decision-making ability? People want control over some choices. Rather than dictate destinations, a human-centered system should offer help: "I have information to support your decision. Would you like a quick recommendation, to review your history, or to adjust your priorities?" Existing infrastructure can become visual guidance. Poorly designed recommendations create obstacles, and users should remain free to disregard them. I generally dislike GPS because I enjoy finding places myself, but I need it when lost: a balance is possible. Conversely, once a device automatically controls home temperature according to need, people probably do not want to calculate how quickly to shovel coal or wood into a fireplace.

**5. Hongyi Wen: Building a working system to collect feedback for user research and test new metrics is challenging for academia. Industry partnerships can provide operational support; MovieLens and GroupLens have also opened their platforms. What would you recommend for future human-centered research on such platforms?**

**Joseph:** I agree that partnerships with commercial, production, or government systems can help. Building a system for just one study is not worthwhile. MovieLens, however, supports many studies: over the years we and other researchers have used its system and data extensively. Universities, especially large research groups, can do this. A decade-long research agenda across multiple topics justifies a platform supporting varied investigations—a genuinely human-centered approach. Government investment is also needed. We have applied to the US National Science Foundation for funding an open news recommendation infrastructure, aiming for thousands of readers using applications we control. Everyone, not just us, could conduct research on it. Open systems and shared information remain rare, and publicly accessible user communities and software are hard to find. This is something we should work toward.
