Recipients of the 2022 STC Jay R. Gould Award for Excellence in Teaching have just been announced. This influential award honors outstanding contributions to technical communication education. Huatong Sun is the first scholar of Chinese heritage to receive it. Her achievement sets an example for Chinese scholars and contributes meaningfully to technical communication.

Peking University's Technical Communication Association interviewed Huatong Sun. We sincerely thank Huatong Sun for supporting our activities and sharing her ideas and experiences, and Zhijun Gao for arranging the interview.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2022-04-12-interview-02.png)

## Professor Huatong Sun

Huatong Sun (Huatong Sun) is an associate professor of digital media design at the University of Washington Tacoma, associate director of the Design Innovation Laboratory, and a director of International Chinese Association of Computer Human Interaction. She earned a bachelor's degree in Chinese literature at Fudan, a master's in technical communication at Michigan Technological University, and a PhD in communication and rhetoric at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute. Her teaching, research, and practice focus on localization, cross-cultural and multicultural design, and global user experience.

This year, Huatong Sun received three research book awards from the Conference on College Composition and Communication, part of the National Council of Teachers of English: the Advancement of Knowledge Award, Research Impact Award, and Best Book in Technical or Scientific Communication. She is the first scholar to receive all three technical communication-related awards simultaneously and the first woman to win Best Book in Technical or Scientific Communication twice.

## Interview Transcript

### Question 1

Congratulations on receiving three research book awards from the US writing association. We understand you are the first scholar to win all three at once. How do you feel about this achievement?

**Answer:**

I feel fortunate that three independent committees recognized my book. There is no undisputed first place in writing, unlike competitions with clear results; humanities awards depend on judgments that can differ. All three committees considered my book the best, and it was the sole winner of each award this year. I am grateful for that recognition. Second, this kind of award recognition matters to me. In a society where racial and cultural ideologies remain prominent, Asian women scholars encounter invisible discrimination and our abilities are not always recognized. Awards help demonstrate what we can do. A Taiwanese colleague and I recently discussed why one award may not suffice to establish our excellence and several seem necessary.

But awards are not everything. My first book also won an award. When writing Cross-Cultural Technology Design, I was an assistant professor without powerful connections or a famous mentor, yet secured an Oxford University Press contract and a US$2,000 advance. I was excited and hoped to write a prizeworthy book, which made me anxious. I later told myself that more than award recognition, I wanted the book still to be on someone's shelf and remain meaningful thirty years later. Winning then ceased to matter as much: I wanted to express my ideas clearly and give them lasting value. So award recognition is an added honor; what matters more is inspiring ourselves and benefiting the discipline.

### Question 2

Could you briefly introduce your latest book, Global Social Media Design: Bridging Differences Across Cultures?

**Answer:**

I specifically asked the cover designer to arrange the title so readers first see Global Design, then social media, then Global Social Media Design. That conveys at least three messages. Today's social media design is highly segmented: platforms and systems enclose people for economic and political reasons. For example, sharing a Taobao link on WeChat is difficult. People become divided into small groups and echo chambers. In a world full of differences in perspectives, how can we design social media that bridges them?

I initially studied cross-cultural design and localization. My second book attempts a move from cross-cultural to global design: how can we connect people better from a global perspective? I proposed a framework of discursive affordances. It examines how micro-level interface design can connect with social and global structures to empower people. Much human–computer interaction and interface design remains superficial, concentrating on sensory experience while neglecting interfaces' significance to people's inner lives and their empowering potential. My framework examines how design can realize these affordances.

The subtitle is Bridging Differences Across Cultures. I have faced cultural differences throughout my life. My family is from Hangzhou, but I was born in Liuzhou, Guangxi. Returning to Hangzhou at twelve felt like coming home, yet I could not speak the dialect and classmates did not consider me local. Later, American students said I spoke with an accent, although I also have an accent in my native Chinese. Writing offered a chance to explore existence, identity, and bridging difference. As I explain in the preface, I felt fortunate to publish these ideas internationally amid such division. Recognition from peers makes me feel even more fortunate.

### Question 3

How did your education lead you from Chinese literature to Western aesthetics and then human–computer interaction? How do you see the connections between them?

**Answer:**

Fudan's Chinese department has an excellent aesthetics tradition. My supervisor, Liyuan Zhu, is a leading Western aesthetics scholar specializing in practical and reception aesthetics. The saying "There are a thousand Hamlets in a thousand people's eyes" emphasizes readers' imagination and creation of new aesthetic worlds. During my first master's year in the United States, I took an engaging usability testing course about user-centered design. Moving from readers to users felt natural given my reception aesthetics background; I wrote a short paper comparing them. I also discovered human–computer interaction then. With classmates studying computing, I took C programming, discrete structures, and interface design. I considered switching to computer science for better job prospects but decided to build on my strengths. human–computer interaction let me stay in technical communication while connecting with computing. When applying for a PhD, I found RPI's human–computer interaction program. RPI was the first US university to offer bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees in technical communication, and several teachers had studied there. I considered it the best program and was determined to attend.

### Question 4

Among your many awards, which has influenced you most, and how?

**Answer:**

The most influential recognition—or most important stepping stone—was selection for the Doctoral Consortium at ACM CHI 2003, the Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems in human–computer interaction. Our university gave graduate students only US$250 annually for travel, far too little for a doctoral student attending conferences. I saw the advertisement for the leading human–computer interaction conference's consortium, which accepted fifteen papers annually and covered accommodation and meals for accepted students. I could not miss the opportunity and worked hard to secure it. Over two days, each student had an hour: thirty minutes presenting and thirty receiving feedback from four expert professors and researchers. I was fortunate to meet my later mentor, Elizabeth Churchill, who was also using the cultural circuit model and offered valuable advice. The recognition helped me enter human–computer interaction and establish professional connections.

### Question 6

How do you view technical communication as a discipline or profession in China and overseas?

**Answer:**

Technical communication has different meanings in China, the United States, and Europe. China distinguishes technical communication from science communication, with the latter more associated with news media. The United States combines them within a broader perspective, while Europe follows different directions again. I recommend approaching technical communication through design thinking: a simple concept can connect many areas. Its different manifestations serve local socioeconomic circumstances, so we need not be confined to one place or time. Technology changes rapidly, and interesting developments happen daily.

### Question 7

What advice or hopes do you have for graduate or undergraduate students who wish to enter technical communication or human–computer interaction, professionally or academically?

**Answer:**

Schiller's idea that art and aesthetics originate in play profoundly influenced me as a Chinese literature student. I was astonished in class, but later research and experience confirmed it. My advice is to find something you enjoy and follow your passion. If you love gaming, play games: they reveal design questions involving interfaces and ideology. Go outside and connect with society; when ordering delivery, observe couriers' lives. Shopping and livestreaming can both relate to human–computer interaction. One advantage of interdisciplinary humanities, technical communication, and human–computer interaction is that, given time, almost anything can become an interesting subject for writing. Having worked in the field for many years, I know young people are its future. I have confidence in you: students who initially seem inexperienced can change remarkably within a few years. Believe in yourselves.
