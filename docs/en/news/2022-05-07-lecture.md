## About the Lecture

Chinese typewriters may seem like short-lived, insignificant technological curiosities, a sideshow to Chinese computing's real story. In fact, they underpin China's contemporary achievements in digital computing and new media and laid foundations for today's IT giants. Predictive text, text corpora, adaptive databases, and even digital graphics were pioneered in mechanical Chinese typewriting. Above all, these supposedly impossible machines showed that modernization did not depend on abolishing Chinese characters, as Lu Xun and others assumed. As one early engineer put it, Chinese characters are innocent.

Chinese typewriters might seem like short-lived, insignificant technological curiosities—a sideshow to the real story of Chinese computing. In fact, they underpin China's contemporary achievements in digital computing and new media and laid foundations for today's Chinese IT juggernaut. Predictive text, text corpora, adaptive databases, and even digital graphics were pioneered in mechanical Chinese typewriting. Above all, Chinese typewriters, machines many assumed impossible to build, showed that Chinese modernization did not, as Lu Xun and others assumed, depend on abolishing character-based Chinese script. As one early engineer phrased it, "Chinese characters are innocent."

## Discussant

### Shumin Zhai

## Shumin Zhai

Dr. Shumin Zhai is a Principal Scientist at Google. He was a Research Staff Member at IBM Almaden from 1996 to 2010; a visiting professor at KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Linkoping University, and Paris-Sud University in 2001–2002; and a visiting chair professor in Tsinghua University's computer science department in 2004. He co-chaired the papers committee of ACM CHI, the ACM human–computer interaction conference, in 2005, and was editor-in-chief of ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction, the ACM human–computer interaction journal, from 2009 to 2015. His papers have received an ACM UIST lasting impact award and an IEEE Computer Society best paper award. He was named an inaugural ACM Distinguished Scientist in 2006, elected to the human–computer interaction Academy and as an ACM Fellow in 2010, and inducted into the University of Toronto Engineering Hall of Distinction in 2020. As of 2021, his academic metrics were an H-index of 76, an i10-index of 186, and 18,658 citations; he held 103 US patents.

Shumin Zhai is a human-computer interaction research scientist at Google where he leads and directs research, design, and development of manual and text input methods and haptics systems. His research career has contributed to both fundamental models and understandings of human-computer interaction and practical user interface designs, inventions and mass-products. He originated and led the SHARK/ShapeWriter project and a start-up company that pioneered the touchscreen swiping-gesture keyboard paradigm, filing the first patents of this paradigm, publishing the first generation of scientific papers and dissertations (by his former Ph.D. student Per Ola Kristensson) of the field, releasing the first word-gesture keyboard in 2004 through IBM Alphaworks, and a top ranked (6th) iPhone app called ShapeWriter WritingPad in 2008. His publications have won the ACM UIST Lasting Impact Award and a IEEE Computer Society Best Paper Award, among others. He served as the 4th Editor-in-Chief of ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction, and frequently contributes to other academic boards and program committees. He received his Ph.D. degree at the University of Toronto in 1995. In 2006, he was named one of ACM's inaugural class of Distinguished Scientists. In 2010 he was named Member of the CHI Academy and Fellow of the ACM.
