From late January to February 2016, I had the opportunity to visit Professor Xiangshi Ren's CHEC laboratory at Kochi University of Technology for a short exchange.

A sense of ease, generosity, and academic freedom.

When tired of reading papers, I could sit anywhere in the spacious laboratory, even on the sofa. When thinking became frustrating, I could clear my mind on the treadmill. When a design idea stalled, I could discuss it with a doctoral student. Nobody dismissed me for being an undergraduate: I could suggest ideas for doctoral projects and stand on others' shoulders to see farther. Nobody withheld knowledge, played politics, or brushed me off. It was a peaceful little Eden with its own rhythm. Sometimes I instinctively spoke Chinese to international classmates, unconsciously assuming they could understand.

You may be bold, but not arrogant or impatient. Repeated discussions were not meant to reject me but to ground my thinking and help me advance steadily in research. Egyptian doctoral student Mahmoud arrived at eight in the morning; Chinese doctoral student Wang Qinglong was still there at ten at night. Dim from Myanmar, Indian postdoctoral researcher Sayan, and Iranian doctoral student Kavous, who enthusiastically returned to do experiments on weekends, all encouraged me through their kindness when I doubted myself. I asked Kavous why he still worked on Sundays. He answered, "Because I like it and I am interested in it." His enthusiasm reminded me of why I had begun: wasn't it all for the love of the work?

I found Indian and Japanese accents particularly difficult to understand, and communication was hardest with Japanese classmates who spoke little English. One evening, I wanted their views on a question. We used two computers and Google Translate to bridge the language gap. I wrote on paper: "Would you share your emotions with strangers if you could not share them with your family?"

The answers surprised me: nobody said yes, and their reasons were difficult to dismiss. Some feared hurting or burdening others; some said they could solve problems themselves, adding that this had worked so far. Others were shy, felt strangers were unsafe, saw no point, or found communicating emotions troublesome. Their common concern was not inconveniencing others. I was astonished—wasn't mutual support valuable? Yet their consideration affected me deeply. In rapidly developing China, how often do we think that way? When upset, we turn to friends or parents, share our feelings, and rarely consider the effect on the listener.

Forty days passed in a blink, leaving me reluctant to go. This quiet town was ideal for focused study, with generous colleagues and excellent research conditions. What could not be achieved here? My thanks go to Qinglong, Zijing, and Fangfang for cooking for me; Adong, Guanghui, and Cancheng for eating my cooking; Mahmoud and Professor Ren for keeping me focused; Dim, whose song moved me to tears; Chaky, Kavous, and Zhenxin for tolerating my questions; and Jinjo, Kume, Sakaue, and everyone who helped. May your minds become clear as crystal, pure within and without, enabling you to pursue your aspirations and accomplish meaningful work.

Completed on the evening of March 10, 2016, at Jilin University
