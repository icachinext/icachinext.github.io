## A New VR Application: Public Speaking Practice

A graduate team from Carnegie Mellon University's Entertainment Technology Center in Pennsylvania created VR Rehearsal. Inspired by another ETC project, the students developed it out of personal interest.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-07-27-article-qlpzydra-04.jpg)

Lee and his team began developing the mobile app in January and launched it on Google Cardboard in late April. Users can upload presentation slides, select a venue, and set a duration. A pixelated audience responds to the speaker's gaze, accompanied by audio feedback. Speakers can use this feedback to adjust their tone, speed, and pacing.

While Lee's team was developing its project, Dominic Barnard and Artur Grzybowski founded a British company working on VR speaking practice. Their VirtualSpeech can be seen as an evolution of VR Rehearsal, offering more settings and richer spatial audio, including coughs and applause from randomly positioned audience members. However, it does not yet include VR Rehearsal's interactive gaze feedback.

## NVIDIA Announces New Eye-Tracking Technology

NVIDIA and German eye-tracking company SMI have introduced a VR display capable of accurate, low-latency eye tracking at 250 Hz. Combining eye tracking with foveated rendering concentrates processing on what the viewer is looking at, substantially reducing GPU demands.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-07-27-article-qlpzydra-05.jpg)

At last year's developer conference, SMI demonstrated a remote eye-tracking system developed with Sony Magic Lab. Working with Samsung, it also released SMI Mobile Eye Tracking HMD Observation for observing and recording visual behavior in virtual environments, and SMI Mobile Eye Tracking HMD Analysis Pro for basic gaze analysis.

A competing product comes from Japan's FOVE, which developed the first eye-tracking VR headset. Two infrared cameras positioned below the lenses track pupils without obstructing the field of view.

## Future iPhones May Offer Glasses-Free 3D

AppleInsider reported on the 20th that a newly disclosed Apple patent concerns simulated 3D imagery. In principle, it could turn iPhone and other Apple displays into stereoscopic screens that do not require 3D glasses.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-07-27-article-qlpzydra-06.jpg)

The patent describes an array of display elements containing secondary subpixel arrays and lens structures that refract light at different angles. A key light-directing component sends stereoscopic light beams toward the viewer to form a 3D image. Cameras, accelerometers, gyroscopes, and GPS could help determine the direction in which light should be sent.

## Apple Projection Patent Enables Gesture-Controlled Games

A newly disclosed Apple patent describes the 3D optical mapping system shown below. A pattern projector (#18) generates patterned beams through an aperture (#29) onto an object such as a hand (#26), while an optical detector (#28) collects reflected light.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-07-27-article-qlpzydra-07.jpg)

A controller (#31) may contain one or more processors. It receives and processes the detector's image data to reconstruct a 3D map of object #26. This map represents the object's surface coordinates, a process described as 3D mapping or reconstruction. The system could be integrated into iPhone, iPad, iPod, and Mac devices.
