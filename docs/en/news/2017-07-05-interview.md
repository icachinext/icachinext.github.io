The age of AI and the Internet of Things has arrived, and human–computer interaction plays a role like air and water: barely noticed, yet everywhere. Some even argue that wherever people exist, there is human–computer interaction.

On June 7, 2017, Chen Wang, deputy director of Xinhua's Converged Media Future Research Institute, interviewed Professor Xiangshi Ren, director of the Center for Human-Computer Interaction at Japan's Kochi University of Technologyhuman–computer interaction institute, lifetime honorary president of ICACHI, and honorary chair of the International Chinese human–computer interaction Conference. Their discussion explored developments in human–computer interaction and human–computer synergy, opening new perspectives on integrating Eastern thought with human–computer interaction.

Professor Xiangshi Ren in an exclusive interview with Zhigu

**human–computer interaction: Eastern Philosophy's Long Absence**

Modest, gentle, sincere, and slightly nervous during the first five minutes of conversation, he embodies a familiar image of an Eastern scholar. As leader of the internationally renowned Kochi University of Technology Center for Human-Engaged Computing (CHEC), the human–computer interaction institute in human–computer interaction, Professor Xiangshi Ren is the university's only non-Japanese professor directing a research institute. He has studied human–computer interaction overseas for nearly 30 years. For the first 20, he attended many international human–computer interaction conferences where most participants came from the West. "Twenty years ago, people in China had not even heard of human–computer interaction," he recalls.

human–computer interaction, also called Human–Computer Interaction or Human–Machine Interaction (HCI or HMI), studies interactions between people and machines or systems, combining computer science, psychology, sociology, graphic design, and industrial design. In 1960, AI pioneer J. C. R. Licklider proposed close human–computer symbiosis, an early foundation for human–computer interfaces. By the early 1980s, technological advances broadened the theory and practice of interfaces, emphasizing cognitive psychology, sociology, other humanistic perspectives, and computer feedback to users. The term human–computer interaction consequently replaced human–computer interface. Since the late 1990s, advances in intelligent technologies have shifted human–computer interaction toward intelligent, multimodal, multimedia, virtual, and collaborative interaction, emphasizing a human-centered approach.

Professor Xiangshi Ren has conducted fundamental and applied human–computer interaction research since 1991 and is a leading Chinese scholar in human–computer interaction. Yet, as he recalls, he encountered only three Chinese scholars at his first international human–computer interaction conference in Atlanta in 1997.

This was more than an absence of Chinese scholars or Eastern thought from one research area. Beyond differences in economic development, modern science and technology have long been understood as grounded in Western philosophy's emphasis on reason and logic. Eastern philosophies, with their focus on inner cultivation, harmony between people and nature, and restraint in transforming the environment, have often seemed too metaphysical and insufficiently practical. From early computing to today's big data and AI, machines' operation through rules, conditions, probability, and statistics reflects Western rationalism. Eastern philosophical ideas have been particularly rare in human–computer interaction research.

"I still felt very lonely then," Professor Xiangshi Ren says.

## Rethinking the Relationship Between People and Machines

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2017-07-05-interview-02.jpg)

Image source: Internet

With today's technological advances, machines' role—or the substance of human–computer interaction—extends well beyond helping humans complete tasks. How much further can algorithms and vast stores of data and knowledge take machines? Can research focused exclusively on machine operating rules achieve deeper human–machine collaboration?

"We must consider what humans truly want, and the relationship between people and machines," Professor Xiangshi Ren replies.

What do humans truly want? This sounds like the philosophical question "Who am I?" Yet are intelligent machines not also part of humanity's search for an answer? human–computer interaction likewise explores our relationship with the world.

Modern technology offers convenience, frees people from tedious labor, and improves effectiveness and productivity. Machines even outperform humans at many tasks. Their influence continues to grow, and increasingly intelligent machines may transform their relationship with people from subordination to symbiosis. But is this what people want? Professor Xiangshi Ren expresses concern: "Technological innovation often accompanies a decline in human capabilities. Long-term reliance on electronic address books can weaken memory, while prolonged use of electronic devices can impair concentration." For example, a study associated with the National Center for Biotechnology Information reported that average attention spans fell from 12 seconds to eight—one second shorter than a goldfish's—between 2000 and 2013 alone. As smart devices develop, attention may become increasingly fragmented. Declining human capabilities could also limit deeper interaction with machines.

Rethinking human–machine relations is essential. Professor Xiangshi Ren says: "Some engineers predict that machines will replace humans. I call that a machine approach. I propose a human approach: starting from our own perspective, do we wish to become machines' slaves? What do we really want? If humans should always remain in control, how should we interact with computers at a higher level?" These questions address technology's fundamental purpose. He argues that we should minimize technology's negative effects and strengthen innate human abilities. In other words, technology should enhance human intelligence and capabilities.

In 2013, Professor Xiangshi Ren proposed Human-Engaged Computing (HEC), describing the organic, synergistic development of human and technological capabilities. Its three components are engaged humans, engaging computers, and synergized interaction. In "Rethinking the Relationship between Humans and Computers," he argues that human–computer interaction researchers must bring human potential and technological performance together in mutually reinforcing development. He also proposes integrating ideas such as the Chinese Doctrine of the Mean into human–computer interaction. Advancing human–computer interaction requires more than research on technology and cognition: abstract human capabilities rooted in the subtle workings of the mind also call for understanding through Eastern philosophy. These ideas have attracted considerable interest among Western scholars.

**human–computer interaction and Human-Engaged Computing: Bringing in Eastern Philosophy**

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2017-07-05-interview-03.jpg)

Image source: Internet

Professor Xiangshi Ren says human–computer interaction currently prioritizes interaction while the two participants—humans and computers—receive less attention. Philosophical reflection, particularly through Eastern philosophy, on human–computer interaction and human–machine relations is rarer still. Finding ways for both sides to progress together and co-evolve is an urgent task for human–computer interaction.

In "Rethinking the Relationship between Humans and Computers," Professor Ren connects the human–computer collaboration envisioned by J. C. R. Licklider and Douglas Engelbart in the 1960s with the Chinese philosophical ideal of balanced moderation: "This balance is complementarity between the interacting parties: their combined capability is much greater than either alone. Humans and computers should be treated proportionately to their respective contributions to overall harmony in a given interaction, rather than invariably being treated equally."

In brief, this is the Doctrine of the Mean. Professor Ren explains: "Neither a purely technology-driven approach nor one concerned only with convenience and pleasing users truly embodies human–computer synergy. Eastern thinking about the right balance can inform interaction between people and machines. Neither side should dominate absolutely, nor must the two invariably carry equal weight. The balance depends on the particular goal, task, situation, and appropriate proportions."

Professor Wei Liu, director of the human–computer interaction and Cognitive Engineering Laboratory at Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, has proposed a related idea: being together. In The Future of Artificial Intelligence: Reflections on Several Important Issues, he argues for a relationship in which people and machines contribute according to their strengths, complement one another, advance together, awaken each other's potential, combine science with imagination and feeling, and achieve mutual enrichment.

Based on this balanced coexistence, Professor Ren's HEC theory focuses on identifying ways to engage people and realize their full potential. He notes that traditional human–computer interaction often confines human potential to cognition and motor control. Eastern philosophy, however, recognizes immeasurable, intangible inner capacities beyond machines' capabilities. He calls these soft skills, including concentration, imagination, inspiration, and empathy.

Einstein described the difference between logic and imagination: "Logic will get you from A to B. Imagination will take you everywhere." In Deep Gesture Sensing: More Than Simple human–computer interaction, Professor Wei Liu likewise notes that a key difference between humans and machines in language and information processing is the ability to connect seemingly unrelated things. A gap remains between machines' abstract representations and decisions based on rules and probability, and distinctively human judgments arising from emotion, insight, and meditation.

These uniquely human soft skills, extending beyond cognition and perception, are the mind's highest products under intense concentration and lie at the heart of Eastern philosophy. Concentration, imagination, and inspiration may correspond more closely to meditative stability, intuition, and sudden realization in Zen. Within technology, Zen's influence is particularly visible in industrial design. Apple founder Steve Jobs practiced Zen, incorporating its ideas, Eastern imagery, and minimalist aesthetics into his technological pursuits and ultimately into Apple's popular products. Similarly, Panos Panay, the creator of Microsoft Surface and an enthusiast of meditation, has repeatedly brought presence and flow into product design—being fully in the present and wholeheartedly engaged, ideas resonating with Eastern philosophy.

Eastern concepts of well-being, holism, and meditation also offer insights for human–computer interaction applications such as smart homes, intelligent enterprise management, and healthcare. Professor Ren says: "Combining Eastern holistic thinking with Western analytical thinking can provide the best way to use computers to meet human needs."

Despite rapid technological progress, Professor Ren believes superintelligent AI may remain beyond our generation and perhaps several generations to come. Yet if humanity wants it, its arrival may simply be a matter of time. Understanding human needs must therefore come first.

"Perhaps people do not want it," he says with a smile.

## human–computer interaction Flourishes in China

When J. C. R. Licklider discussed human–computer symbiosis in 1960, he outlined three stages of development. Professor Xiangshi Ren describes our present stage in the age of AI and ubiquitous connectivity as integration, or Human-Engaged Computing.

In recent years, human–computer interaction has appeared increasingly often in scholarly discussions and national strategic planning. In 2006, China incorporated Chinese information processing and intelligent human–computer interaction technologies into its information industry's 11th Five-Year Science and Technology Development Plan and long-term outline for 2020, making human–computer interaction a nationally promoted technological and industrial field. Western dominance in frontier areas such as AI and big data has also shifted: by 2012, China's AI patent applications had surpassed those of the United States, giving it the world's largest number of AI patents. Continuing technological breakthroughs have expanded human–computer interaction research and applications in China.

Professor Xiangshi Ren describes the future of human–computer interaction in China as flourishing: "During my 30 years abroad, the field received relatively little attention in China for the first 20 years. But over the last decade, particularly the past five years, human–computer interaction has developed remarkably rapidly."

To improve exchanges among Chinese human–computer interaction scholars in China and overseas and advance human–computer interaction, Professor Xiangshi Ren founded International Chinese Association of Computer Human Interaction in 2012, with annual international conferences. At Chinese CHI 2017, Xinhua's Converged Media Future Research Institute received the conference's sole Best Paper Award.

Professor Xiangshi Ren commends this achievement, observing that media organizations have advantages in human–computer interaction research and development: "You have actual users and can obtain more feedback. Today's media must keep pace with the times and recognize trends, or risk being left behind, as Nokia and Kodak were. How to transform more quickly is a question worth considering."

Professor Ren also stresses that universities should recognize the importance of human–computer interaction early, develop related courses, or establish research institutes. Although curricula may contain many gaps, he asks, "Are gaps not opportunities?" Universities should bring together underused equipment, talent, and other resources to explore frontier technologies, while the industry should unite to advance human–computer interaction. "In technology, we should always think ahead." (By Jinglan Xu)

Future Researcher in This Issue: Chen Wang

Chen Wang is deputy director and chief researcher at Xinhua's Converged Media Future Research Institute, focusing on user experience research using wearable devices. After a bachelor's degree in communications engineering at Nanjing University of Posts and Telecommunications, she earned a master's in electrical engineering at Delft University of Technology and pursued doctoral research at the Netherlands' national research institute for mathematics and computer science, specializing in physiological computing for user experience assessment. She combines human–computer interaction, data science, hardware design, and computer networking in a distinctive research approach connecting scholarship with innovative industries. Her physiological sensors have been deployed in cinemas, museums, theaters, and distributed learning environments. Her work has appeared at leading conferences including ACM CHI, ACM CSCW, NordiCHI, PhyCS, and QoMEX.
