## Book Recommendation: Human-Engaged Computing

Xiangshi Ren，Zhiyong Fu，Xiaojuan Ma，Jianmin Wang，Huatong Sun，Xiaojun Yuan，Chen Wang

Authors

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2024-09-27-article-asst8ect-01.png)

Centered on the concept of Human-Engaged Computing, this book synthesizes developments in human–computer interaction and offers insights into its future.

The book comprises 11 chapters in two parts: theory and technology. Chapters 1–4 introduce the theoretical framework of Human-Engaged Computing. Reviewing the history of human–computer interaction, they trace the origins of Human-Engaged Computing, examine technology's purposes and developmental trends, and clarify the scope of the Human-Engaged Computing framework. Chapters 5–11 address technology through an HEC perspective, connecting artificial intelligence, digital mindfulness, autonomous driving, information interaction, design futures, and cross-cultural design with Human-Engaged Computing and offering related cases and perspectives.

The book is intended for young students and researchers across disciplines, especially those seeking innovation beyond established frameworks, encouraging reflection on their research positions and future directions.

### Dongyi Chen

## Foreword I

From human–computer interaction to Human-Engaged Computing

Looking back on fifty years of research, I have been fortunate to witness human–computer interaction grow from its beginnings into a technological cornerstone of the personal computer era. Its achievements now reach countless households, enabling everyone to use electronic devices and the Internet freely. In recent years, I have also seen academia and industry in China increasingly value human–computer interaction. When I read Professor Xiangshi Ren's Rethinking the Relationship Between Humans and Computers, I recognized Human-Engaged Computing (HEC) as a rare theoretical exploration in HCI. Several years later, I am delighted to congratulate the authors on the publication of Human-Engaged Computing. This is a major event for China's and the world's human–computer interaction communities, and it has prompted me to study and reflect deeply. Rich in ideas, theory, methods, and practice, the book deserves to mark a turning point. I hope it will become not only an academic monograph but also a textbook for required university courses.

Drawing on this book, I would like to share several observations on the current development of human–computer interaction.

First, the book presents the ideas and achievements of Bush, Licklider, and Engelbart, tracing the intellectual history of augmenting human intelligence. This is both HEC's starting point and the essence of human–computer interaction. In the past sixty years, human–computer interaction has essentially pursued Bush's MEMEX vision, opening the personal computer era and later adding the Internet to address the organization of human knowledge. Yet questions of human existence and human–computer relationships beyond that knowledge base have received little attention. Guided by the augmentation of human intelligence, the authors develop an integrated theoretical system and methodology through extensive research and engineering practice.

Second, human–computer interaction and AI both study humans and should be sister disciplines within computing, yet historically they have competed for resources and developed separately. AI ultimately requires human-centered applications and experiments, while humanity understands no more than 20% of the brain. Even systems matching human or brain-like performance therefore remain fundamentally limited in their contributions to human well-being. The technological conditions for augmenting human intelligence are, however, available. I hope the two disciplines can develop together.

Third, human–computer interaction has flourished in the 21st century, but beneath that prosperity I increasingly notice a shortage of ideas and theory. human–computer interaction is not a single technology or algorithm: it concerns the application of computing and technology throughout human society and is crucial to humanity's future. It should be a guiding discipline for information, communications, and other fields, receiving the attention and development it deserves. I also feel that human–computer interaction remains insufficiently healthy, both in China and internationally, and has limited academic influence, partly because the human–computer interaction community misjudges its own position. Existing HCI textbooks largely follow earlier thinking and lack theoretical depth; this book offers a systematic account of human–computer relationships and a holistic understanding of people.

Fourth, readers should consider this book in relation to the survival of human civilization. New technologies profoundly affect human development, and every technology has positive and negative effects. human–computer interaction is important because it forms the interface between new technologies and human activities. Its healthy development can realize technology's benefits, avoid its harms, and enable humanity and technology to develop harmoniously.

Fifth, Western civilization has dominated technological development over the past century, including today's AI. Western thinking suited an age of certainty, but Eastern thinking is beginning to reveal its value as we enter an uncertain era. Human-Engaged Computing brings Eastern and Western thought together, articulating a holistic perspective and the dynamically changing doctrine of the mean from Eastern civilization, rather than merely coordinating technical approaches across fields from a Western perspective. It argues that technology should augment mental capacities and advance synergistically with people. Its orientation and objectives differ from recent technology-driven concepts based on industrial-era evaluation criteria. As Eastern civilization becomes increasingly important, the center of human–computer interaction, intimately connected with civilization, will shift toward China.

The publication of Human-Engaged Computing is timely: rapid historical change is exposing overlooked risks, while Professor Xiangshi Ren brings more than thirty years of deep understanding of human–computer interaction and technology's nature. We are entering an age of diverse computing paradigms. Synergistic computing will not be the only future paradigm, but it will be among the most important and widespread, requiring refinement through practice and development through application. I hope the book's publication inspires a high-level academic forum for rigorous, systematic study and open, substantive discussion of HEC. By pooling ideas and exploring cases that embody its principles, we can establish a research and development paradigm for the next wave of human–computer interaction.

## Foreword II

Unity of Mind and Matter, Inseparability of Principle and Practice

As a professional historian who calls himself computer-illiterate, I never expected to read a monograph on human–computer interaction and synergy with such engagement.

The first highlight is the authors' fundamental reflection on the Dao. In an increasingly materialized world, swept along by technological supremacy, we can lose sight of technology's direction. We have little time to consider how human society should exist, human–computer relationships, what we truly want, or the essence of being human. The authors sound an alarm with significance not only for human–computer interaction but for science, technology, and academia as a whole. Yet they do not stop at philosophical speculation: they actively explore feasible technical solutions grounded in everyday life. This develops and improves practical instruments under the guidance of fundamental principles.

Another highlight is the authors' thinking about elevating the mind. Unlike ancient Greek mind–body dualism, they draw actively on traditional Chinese culture, integrating Eastern philosophy into conventional human–computer interaction oriented toward cognition and the body. They understand Eastern thought's holistic and introspective qualities and argue that technology must ultimately support inner breakthroughs and help people attain a nonmaterial ideal state. Every major transformation of mind, consciousness, and thought has in fact brought significant social and technological change, as in the Western Renaissance and China's reform, opening-up, and emancipation of thought.

One reason the authors could write this seemingly unconventional scientific monograph is their international engagement and academic experience spanning East and West, enabling them to bridge modern technology and Eastern traditions. Another intellectual source is the principal authors' embodied, introspective practices. We have all benefited greatly from meditation, which deepens my resonance with the book.

This is a scholarly yet accessible scientific book. I hope more younger readers and readers outside the field will discover and enjoy it.

## Foreword III

A Work That Points the Way Forward for Computing

Reading Human-Engaged Computing by Professor Xiangshi Ren and colleagues was refreshing and deeply inspiring. It reflects the authors' sustained thinking about computing's future and the understanding, refinement, and exploration gained through years of work in human–computer interaction.

The authors approach human–computer interaction from the distinctive perspective of human–computer relationships, explaining the background, significance, concepts, and framework of human–computer synergy. Reviewing the history and achievements of human–computer interaction, they identify current challenges in human–computer interaction and argue that existing human–computer interaction paradigms and technologies cannot meet future computing needs, establishing the importance of Human-Engaged Computing. Limited early capabilities and applications encouraged people to treat computers as specialized tools. Yet computers differ fundamentally from major earlier inventions such as steam engines and electric motors. Traditional ICT and human–computer interaction theories have not addressed human mental life adequately. This insight provides important inspiration and direction for the future of human–computer interaction and AI.

The section on mindfulness meditation and interaction particularly interested me. For several years I have studied meditation's relationship to autonomic regulation, discussed mindfulness, physical and mental health, and mindful computing with Professor Xiangshi Ren, and attended his online seminars. Incorporating mindfulness meditation into human–computer synergy is innovative.

The central aim of Human-Engaged Computing is to achieve a highly synergistic relationship between people and computers, enabling mutual advancement and addressing complex external and internal human problems, including developing the mind and recovering one's essential nature. As a longtime wearable computing researcher, I consider synergy through that lens. The concepts, attributes, and models proposed for wearable computing over twenty years ago align remarkably closely with human–computer synergy. Wearable computing emphasizes human-centeredness, collaboration, symbiosis, and integration, seeking to augment intelligence, perception, and physical capability. From this perspective, wearable computing may best embody Human-Engaged Computing and realize its goals.

Reexamining human–computer interaction and human–computing relationships through a new computing ecosystem perspective, and discussing synergistic AI, this book points the way forward for computing.
