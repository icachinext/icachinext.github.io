## Reflections on HCI in Product Development

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2015-06-30-article-rvvtggga-02.png)

**Author: Yihsiu Chen, HTC Creative Labs**

Dr. Yihsiu Chen has rich experiences in user experience of communication products and services. He has contributed to the design of products ranging from network-based communication services, PBX systems, IVR applications to smartphones, serving companies such as AT&T, Avaya and Verizon Wireless. He is currently the Director of User Research in HTC Creative Labs.

## Introduction

This April, I had the honor of sharing experiences from industrial product development at ICACHI's annual conference. I call these reflections rather than a systematic account because my thoughts range widely without a single clear thread. Afterward, a student told me: "Your talk was inspiring—more like an essay than a research paper." Whether that was praise or criticism is worth pondering.

## 1

Although I studied social psychology in graduate school, I never taught after completing my doctorate. My interest in HCI led me straight to AT&T Bell Laboratories, and I have remained in telecommunications ever since. Working on many user experience projects in product development has given me some insights. Yet this is an interdisciplinary industry whose practitioners come from varied backgrounds and experiences. Even when our work is similar, our definitions of HCI differ. There is considerable disagreement even within a professional society such as ACM SIGCHI.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2015-06-30-article-rvvtggga-03.png)

In industrial product development, HCI is often an unfamiliar term; people speak instead of usability, user experience (UX), or user interface design. Their purpose is to improve people's interactions with artifacts by understanding psychological processes. This intention has existed since humans first made things. It is neither new nor a consequence of the invention of computers. When our ancestors shaped stone tools and made fire, a torch needed a long enough handle not to burn the hand, and its grip could be rounded with a stone blade for comfort. What are such accumulated practices if not design and usability? Technological progress and product complexity have made us forget that these considerations are basic requirements, rather than fashionable imported terms and standards. We say comfortable clothing is "easy to wear," rather than "highly usable with a good experience," but mean the same thing. A product that fails to meet user needs is unusable, not merely inconvenient.

As an element of product development, HCI must not be defined so narrowly that a computer is required. Whenever a product involves human psychological processes, HCI knowledge can apply, whether the interaction is simple or complex. Alternatively, HCI provides theoretical foundations for user experience professionals, with applications across many kinds of product development.

These theories and applications come down to understanding users and designing products that communicate how they should be used. Designing scissors requires understanding hand size, movement, finger positions, and opening and closing motions, then adding appropriate constraints so the hand naturally grasps and uses them. Design is communication, and communication comes from understanding the other party. Don Norman explained this clearly in The Design of Everyday Things, which I translated into Chinese for Yuan-Liou Publishing in Taipei, so I will not elaborate here. Yet scissors are structurally simple: they require an understanding of a hand rather than a whole person. This raises a question: what kind of beings are we in the eyes of our tools? How does the person a tool perceives differ from the person other people perceive?

## 2

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2015-06-30-article-rvvtggga-04.png)

If scissors see us as a hand and shoes see us as feet, a computer may see a creature with eyes, hence its screen; fingers, hence its keyboard and mouse; ears, hence its speakers; and a mouth, hence speech input. It does not care whether we have a body. This partial understanding limits what computers can do for us and how we can use them. Moreover, everything computers know is taught or entered by us, and their understanding of people is fragmented, unlike our understanding of one another.

With smartphones and Internet services, Google, QQ, Taobao, Amazon, Facebook, Baidu, and Sina Weibo may have collected more information about us than we know about one another. Yet this information remains an aggregation of partial observations of behavior. It may persuade us to buy more on Taobao and pay seamlessly through Alipay, but does that really constitute understanding a person? It still differs from another person's understanding of us, even a stranger's.

Conversely, how do we perceive products? Take smartphones: if we already entrust so much to them—who still remembers other people's phone numbers?—why can we not trust them to manage themselves? Why do we need so many phone-management apps or worry about batteries draining, storage filling with junk, and mobile data disappearing? Our smartphone research repeatedly finds that users do not feel at ease with their devices.

Why do they feel uneasy? Is it complexity? Many things in modern life are enormously complex, yet we confidently use them without considering that complexity. Electricity is a good example. Generation and distribution involve sophisticated, carefully monitored systems carrying power from varied sources to local substations and into our homes. Mishandling this process could be disastrous. Yet every day we plug in appliances using a very simple mental model: plugging in supplies power, unplugging stops it, and electricity is electricity. Except where supply is inadequate or unreliable, people do not worry whether an outlet will deliver power at the right voltage.

Cars provide another example. Few drivers understand their complex structure, nor do they need to: the car should start, move, and avoid collisions. Telephones, running water, and television offer similar examples throughout daily life. Why are we comfortable with them? Experience tells us that these mental models work and can be relied upon. Psychology has extensive research on heuristics, or mental shortcuts. If a simple model is reliable, a more complex one is unnecessary. This makes complex things seem simple enough to trust.

Yes: trust. We believe an appliance will work when plugged in because we trust the electrical system and socket. We need not maintain the socket or fear it will fail tomorrow. Smart products, in contrast, are difficult to trust. Their batteries are short-lived, connections unreliable, and functions so numerous that they require complicated mental models. This is why many seem detached from everyday needs: they look complex, may not work every time, and make us feel we must understand what they are doing, although we often cannot. Many fail in the market; with necessities such as smartphones, we use them while complaining.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2015-06-30-article-rvvtggga-05.png)

This distrust is also reflected in smartphones' excessive settings. Unable to identify the settings users need or dynamically choose suitable ones, developers transfer responsibility to users. Anxious users gain a sense of control and reassurance through those settings, but the resulting experience remains fragmented. An important task is to use HCI methods to identify a simple mental model that integrates smart products and guides innovative experiences.

We also see that those leading innovation are often designers rather than HCI or user experience researchers. If HCI is so important, why do its researchers so often fail to lead major innovations? I cannot offer convincing statistics or a definitive explanation, only some observations.

## 3

In Design-Driven Innovation, Roberto Verganti argues that radical innovation does not come from users, and that user-centered approaches do not question existing meanings and may even reinforce them. This echoes Don Norman's claim that human-centered design leads to incremental rather than radical innovation. For someone trained in psychology who continually considers psychological processes in product development, this is difficult to accept.

Examining this claim requires considering the complexity of product development.

What does this image represent? Whatever methods researchers use to observe human phenomena, they find only pieces of a puzzle. We therefore need different methods to study the same phenomenon and uncover different pieces. We must also infer the whole from limited pieces while tolerating noise. Most people recognize an elephant in this example, despite the tiger's ears and cow's markings. Insight that identifies the essential pattern without being distracted by noise helps establish a product's direction early.

Here product development differs from scientific inquiry. One decisive experiment can overturn an entire scientific theory, but product development cannot always stop because of one or two contradictory observations or wait for users to validate an innovative direction.

Such insight is a leap in reasoning, what Jon Kolko calls abductive thinking. It often produces a plausible bold conjecture: a hypothesis nobody anticipated, yet one that seems entirely reasonable once articulated. This sounds unusual and is often uncomfortable for researchers trained in data analysis, but it is essential to radical innovation. Even radical innovation does not arise from nothing; rather, its evidence cannot yield conclusions through analysis and induction alone. Supporting radical innovation through research is a major challenge in product research.

## 4

After these wide-ranging reflections, what should HCI professionals do in product development? I offer several suggestions:

First, broaden your perspective. Does HCI study a whole person or a collection of fragmented data? We must consider cultural and social perspectives rather than fixate on partial evidence.

Second, do not restrict your role. Traditional HCI researchers synthesize and analyze observations, interpret results, and follow scientific conventions. In product development, however, a researcher is only one of many interpreters. Other participants bring their own perspectives, and no single set of rules necessarily applies. Fukuzawa Yukichi wrote that heaven creates no person above or below another. I would add that God did not create the world above or below a significance threshold of 0.05. HCI researchers can play broad and varied roles.

Third, bridge deductive and abductive thinking. Explore ways to collect data and construct meaning through more expansive perspectives and imaginative connections.

Fourth, keep people at the center. Understanding people is the distinctive value that HCI researchers bring.

I welcome comments and advice from fellow association members on this short essay.
