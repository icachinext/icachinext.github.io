Winter vacation has come again. The third undergraduate year is exciting and unsettling: should I pursue graduate study or start working? That question seems no simpler than whether the chicken or egg came first. An opportunity for a short exchange at Kochi University of Technology's Human-Engaged Computing institute in Japan offered a different path. I chose to immerse myself in quiet Kochi. I had been moving too quickly, feeling distracted and ungrounded. Thinking of the bustle and worries surrounding New Year at home, I wanted to cross the ocean and experience another reality. Perhaps, as a young woman, I did not have to push myself so hard, but I wanted to live authentically and freely, with my feet on the ground and a steadier sense of myself.

I had heard that Japanese and German people approached things carefully and seriously, but had never felt this firsthand. On arrival, the airport terminal's quiet surprised me. People were not silent; they simply lowered their voices to avoid disturbing others. Staff smiled without any sense of social hierarchy. Even without a common language, looking lost was enough for someone to offer help. Although flight attendants at home also smile, I often sensed distance; ANA's attendants made me feel genuinely welcome. A young woman traveling alone abroad for the first time, unable to speak the language, feels considerable anxiety—not only for herself but for how her parents would cope if anything happened.

I call this a smile of harmony: both infectious and deeply cultivated. Why do people at home seem so reluctant to smile? A smile need not conceal an agenda; it can simply transmit joy. The serious faces on buses and subways make me wonder why we add to life's burdens. I enjoy smiling and seeing others smile. I believe smiles can connect people throughout the world.

While changing flights, I tried unsuccessfully to connect to Wi-Fi so I could reassure my family. A Japanese man nearby enthusiastically helped. Using Google Translate, he wrote detailed instructions and repeatedly checked that I understood. Even when I, the person seeking help, became impatient, he continued carefully explaining and translating step after step.

On the subway and while shopping, I admired the respect shown to disabled people. Thoughtful design was everywhere, expressing quiet care and making independent daily life possible. Subway toilets included separate facilities for men, women, and disabled passengers. Many passengers, young and old, were absorbed in books; smartphones seemed almost out of place. One gray-haired man, probably in his seventies, balanced a book on his left knee and a pen in his right hand, occasionally jotting something down.

This reminded me of reading books on buses at home. I often felt watched and kept my head down beneath my baseball cap, as though I should be embarrassed. I imagined people thinking I studied too much. Really, I simply did not want to waste those spare minutes. There is so much I do not understand; without asking questions and learning, how could I ever understand more?

Leaving the subway and walking upstairs, I noticed everyone keeping to the right in a quiet, unspoken agreement. There was no jostling; it looked almost rehearsed. I had not seen such consistent behavior even in Beijing, and it made me feel we risked falling behind. While some people at home remained trapped in narrow nationalism, Japan was quietly advancing. The Japanese students in the laboratory were equally kind. When a master's student heard that I wanted to buy a Japanese manga drawing guide for my roommate, he brought out his own books and invited me to choose one as a gift.

Older Japanese people dress and make themselves up carefully before going out, expressing neatness, dignity, and respect for themselves and others. In a drugstore, I struggled to identify brands and products. An older woman nearby looked stern, with a pale face and bright lipstick, and I hesitated to approach her. When I finally asked, her face immediately lit up with a smile. She spoke no English, and I spoke no Japanese, so I showed pictures. She found a sales assistant who spent more than an hour helping me locate everything. Most items were inexpensive and varied, but he never became impatient or tried to upsell me. He simply accompanied me from shelf to shelf. I was deeply moved and would have given him a 200-percent service rating.

We once visited an elementary school near the university. Even though it was just an ordinary class presentation open to visitors, the principal came to take photographs. The corridor displayed balanced meal plans; we learned that printed menus, including nutrients, calories, and allergens, were sent to parents a month in advance. This was a rural school in a city with one of Japan's lowest average incomes. Children were also accustomed to the cold, playing outside in shorts while I still wore padded clothing. Restaurants served iced rather than hot water, and lukewarm dishes and soups left me feeling chilled after meals.

Quiet and clean are the words I associate with Japan. Its considerate everyday design shows respect for people. Everyone conscientiously serves others through their work, offering much that we can learn from. University doors had dampers, closing silently even when opened widely; I was happy not to have to close them gingerly to avoid disturbing students. Soft flooring muffled footsteps. Once, after a long wait for noodles at a nearby restaurant, a piece of meat fell onto the counter just before my dish was served. The server immediately prepared another portion rather than hiding or overlooking the mishap.

![Article image](https://media.icachi.org/news/2016-03-17-student-02.jpg)

Each morning, opening the curtains revealed lush greenery rather than sparse, yellowing saplings. The mountains were so green that their paths disappeared, and the nearby Monobe River could be heard flowing. A good mood arrived instantly. Happiness can be that simple: a morning breeze clearing away the night's heaviness. By humbly appreciating other people's strengths and learning from other countries, we can avoid complacency and keep improving.

Perhaps we ask little of life: living things have spirit, flowers bloom and fade, butterflies dance. A peaceful place and a quiet moment can make each encounter meaningful and each thought clear. To watch the moon reflected in a thousand rivers and clouds travel across the boundless sky—what joy that would bring.

Completed on the evening of March 10, 2016, at Jilin University
